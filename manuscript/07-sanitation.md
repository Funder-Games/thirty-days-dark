# Chapter 7 · Sanitation

> The grid can be down for a month and nobody need get ill. That outcome is decided by where the waste goes and whether hands get washed.

[IMAGE: Cutaway of a household sanitation set-up — a bucket toilet with a seat and a covered pail of sawdust beside it, a tippy-tap and soap at the door, a screened compost bay and a dug pit downhill, with the well drawn at a distance on higher ground]

## Do This Now

1. **Stop flushing.** With no mains pressure a flush pours away drinking water, and a backed-up sewer comes back into the house. Say the rule out loud so nobody forgets at two in the morning.
2. **Make one toilet, now.** A sturdy bucket, a seat unscrewed from another loo or a shaped plank, and a covered pail of dry cover material standing beside it.
3. **Find cover material before dark.** Sawdust, dry soil, wood ash, shredded paper, dry leaves, coir. Two pails of it, kept dry.
4. **Put a hand-washing station at the toilet door** — water, soap, something to catch the runoff. No soap, no toilet.
5. **Keep urine out of the rest** if you can manage it. On its own it barely smells; mixed in, it is what makes the stink.
6. **Choose the pit site while it is light.** Downhill of the house, a long way from any well, spring or stream, and not where the children play.
7. **Label two bins** — burnables and buryables — and keep food waste out of both.
8. **Give anyone ill their own pail,** their own soap and their own towel.
9. **Write the rota on the door:** who empties, who covers, who scrubs. Today's names, in pencil.

Then walk the ground once more and choose where the second toilet goes, because one is never enough.

## The Craft

Sanitation is the least glamorous chapter in this book and the one most likely to keep your household on its feet. In any long outage the illness that arrives is almost never exotic. It is ordinary stomach illness, moving from one person's gut to another person's mouth by a short and entirely preventable route, and it arrives in the second week when the first week's discipline has slipped.

The good news is that the whole problem yields to a handful of dull habits. You do not need plumbing, chemicals or expertise. You need somewhere to go, something to cover it with, soap at the door, and a rota that outlives everybody's enthusiasm.

### The only diagram that matters

Public-health people draw the whole of sanitation as one picture, and it is worth sketching on the back of an envelope for the household to see. Waste leaves a body, and it can get back into a mouth by five roads: fluids, fingers, flies, fields and food. Every job in this chapter is a barrier across one of those roads.

| Road | What it looks like at home | The barrier |
|---|---|---|
| Fluids | Seepage into a well; a flooded pit; a rinsed bucket by the stream | Site the toilet away from water; treat all drinking water |
| Fingers | The latrine door handle, then the bread | Soap and water at the toilet, every time |
| Flies | A fly off an uncovered pit, onto the butter | Lids, cover material, fly mesh, covered food |
| Fields | Soil carried in on boots; unripe compost on salad | Boots off; never fresh waste on food crops |
| Food | Hands, water and flies arriving at the kitchen | Wash hands before cooking; cover everything |

Two barriers beat one, and cheap barriers repeated beat a clever barrier used sometimes. The toilet and the soap are the two that carry the household.

### Your first toilet, in the first hour

Do not overthink the first one. The good dry toilet is one of the oldest designs there is, and you can build it from a bucket in ten minutes.

Take a sturdy pail with a decent rim. Set a real toilet seat on it if you can find one to unscrew, or cut an oval in a plank and sand the edge. Stand it somewhere private with a door that shuts. Beside it put a second, covered pail of dry cover material and a scoop.

The rule of use is one line long: **cover every deposit completely.** A good handful of sawdust or dry soil over everything, every time, so that when you look into the pail you see cover material and not waste. Done properly the pail does not smell, because smell is what happens when waste sits wet and open to the air. Done lazily it will drive the household out of the room by the second day, and then people start going in the garden, which is exactly what you are trying to prevent.

Empty the pail before it is heavy — two-thirds is the sensible limit, since a full pail is a spill waiting to happen and a job nobody wants to volunteer for. Carry it with two hands, tip it into the pit or the compost bay, and give it a rinse with greywater and a long-handled brush kept for that job alone.

> **What I Wish I'd Stocked** — A second bucket, so one can be in use while the other stands rinsed and drying. A clip-on camping toilet seat. A bale of sawdust or compressed wood pellets from the pet shop. Heavy-duty liners. Two boxes of plain soap. A bottle of unscented bleach kept apart from the kitchen cleaners. Rubber gloves. A long-handled brush and a spade that are used for nothing else, both painted a colour nobody will mistake.

### Choosing the toilet you will live with for a month

The bucket gets you through the first night. Within a couple of days, decide what the household is actually going to use, because building it properly is a morning's work and rebuilding it in week three is miserable.

| Option | Good for | The catch |
|---|---|---|
| Dry bucket toilet | Indoors, night, the elderly, cold and wet weather | Somebody must empty it, daily |
| Urine-diverting bucket | Almost no smell; urine becomes fertiliser | Needs a separate bottle or funnel and a little discipline |
| Shallow trench | Groups, gardens, a few days at a time | Short life; must be covered after every use |
| Deep pit with a slab | A household for many months | Digging; siting must be right first time |
| Camping toilet with chemicals | Comfort in the first week | The chemical runs out, and the contents still need somewhere to go |
| Existing WC, hand-flushed | Familiar, private, already plumbed | Only if the sewer or septic tank is genuinely still working |

That last one deserves a straight answer, because everybody asks it. If your house runs to a septic tank on your own land and the tank is not full, you can keep using the lavatory by pouring a bucket of greywater briskly into the pan to force the trap. If you are on a mains sewer, it may work for a while and it may not: sewers depend on pumping stations, and pumping stations need power. The warning sign is a slow drain, a gurgle from the shower or a smell from the lowest gully outside. At that first sign, stop, and go to the bucket. A sewer that backs up into a bathroom is a bad week you cannot undo.

### Siting and digging a pit

Where you put it matters more than how well you build it. Put it downhill and downwind of the house and of anywhere food is prepared, then check it against four figures.

| Distance rule | Figure | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Toilet to any well, borehole, spring or stream | at least 30 m, further in sandy or fissured ground | [src: Sphere — Sphere Handbook: Minimum Standards in Humanitarian Response, 2018] |
| Pit bottom above the water table | at least 1.5 m | [src: Sphere — Sphere Handbook: Minimum Standards in Humanitarian Response, 2018] |
| Toilet to the homes it serves | no more than 50 m | [src: Sphere — Sphere Handbook: Minimum Standards in Humanitarian Response, 2018] |
| People sharing one toilet | 20 at most | [src: Sphere — Sphere Handbook: Minimum Standards in Humanitarian Response, 2018] |

The water-table rule means never digging into wet ground or below the level of a nearby stream. The distance-to-home rule looks like the soft one and is not: a toilet too far away is a toilet nobody walks to in the dark and the rain, and the whole scheme fails on that one human fact.

Reckon on a pit roughly a metre square. A cubic metre of pit takes a household of four a good many months, so dig as deep as you can dig safely and no deeper. That last word is the safety rule of the whole chapter: **never dig deeper than you can climb out of.** Anything past shoulder depth needs shoring with boards, a second person at the top, and a ladder or a knotted rope left in place. Collapsing soil is heavier than people expect and kills quickly. Fence or cover an open pit the moment you stop work, before you go for a cup of tea, and never leave one open where a child or an animal walks after dark.

Over the pit goes a slab you can wash — old floorboards and a sheet of ply will do, or a poured slab if you have cement — with a hole and a tight lid. Around that goes a cubicle, and the cubicle deserves more care than it usually gets: a door that latches from the inside, a hook for a lantern, a shelf, and a roof. Dignity is what makes a latrine get used.

> **Village Hack** — Site the village latrines together before anyone digs their own. Two well-built shared latrines with a named keeper beat nine holes in nine gardens, especially where houses sit close and wells sit between them. Work back from the table above: one toilet for every twenty people, and none of them a long walk from a bed. Separate ones for men and for women, each latching from the inside, with a lamp on the path — otherwise women and girls will avoid them after dark, and avoidance is a health problem.

### Running it so people will use it

A latrine is a habit with a building around it. Post the rules where they can be read from the seat.

- **Cover after every use.** A scoop of ash, sawdust or dry soil. Ash suits a pit particularly well, since it dries the surface and discourages flies.
- **Lid down.** A pit without a lid is a fly factory and a smell pump.
- **Nothing in it but waste, paper and cover.** No nappies, no rags, no sanitary products, no rubbish. Anything that will not rot steals volume from a pit you had to dig by hand.
- **Paper in its own lidded bin** if you want the pit to last, burned or buried later.
- **A vent pipe** running from the pit up past the cubicle roof, painted black and capped with fly mesh, turns the whole thing into a chimney: air is drawn down through the seat and out of the pipe, taking the smell with it, and flies fly up towards the light and die against the mesh.
- **Scrub the slab and the seat daily** with soapy water, and keep the brush at the latrine.
- **Name a keeper, with a deputy.** Same as water: what belongs to everybody gets checked by nobody.

> **Climate note (hot)** — Heat multiplies both smell and flies, and the margin for slackness disappears. Use a deeper layer of cover material, shade the cubicle with matting or a tarpaulin so it does not become an oven nobody will enter, empty the bucket toilet daily rather than when it is full, and keep the hand-washing water in shade so it stays pleasant to use. Do the emptying at dawn while it is cool.

### Handwashing: the cheapest medicine in the book

If you do only one thing in this chapter, put soap and water where hands are dirtiest, at the toilet door and at the kitchen door, and keep them topped up like a religious duty. Soap works by lifting grease and dirt off skin and letting the water carry it away, which is why the mechanics matter as much as the ingredient.

Rub with soap for twenty seconds, covering backs of hands, between fingers, thumbs and under the nails, then rinse in clean running water and dry on your own cloth or in the air [src: CDC — When and How to Wash Your Hands, 2022]. Running water means poured or flowing, never a shared bowl: a basin of standing water becomes a soup that everyone shares within an hour.

Wash at five moments, and teach them as a list: after the toilet, after cleaning up a child, before preparing food, before eating, and before feeding anyone else. Children learn this faster than adults if you make it a routine with a song and a reachable tap.

If soap runs out, wood ash or clean sand rubbed on wet hands with the same twenty seconds of friction is the accepted stand-in, and it is far better than a rinse. Alcohol hand rub is a useful pocket backup at a strength of at least 60 per cent alcohol, but it does not work well on visibly dirty hands, and it is weak against the viruses that cause vomiting outbreaks [src: CDC — When and How to Use Hand Sanitizer, 2021]. Soap and water first, always; gel is the thing you carry to the field.

One litre of water, poured in a thin stream from a bottle with a small hole in the cap, will wash a dozen pairs of hands properly. That is the entire budget argument, and it is why the hygiene allowance is not the one to cut: reckon on two to six litres per person per day for basic hygiene on top of drinking water [src: WHO — Technical Notes on Drinking-water, Sanitation and Hygiene in Emergencies, 2013].

> **Day-X Challenge** — Day five: switch the house to the bucket toilet and the tippy-tap for forty-eight hours while everything else stays normal. Weigh or count the cover material used, time how long a pail lasts, and see who forgets to wash. You will learn more about your household's real weak point in two days than in a month of planning, and you will find out whether your cover material supply is a fortnight's or an afternoon's.

### Greywater, dishes and laundry

Washing water is not sewage and should not be treated as if it were, but let it stand and it turns into a mosquito nursery and a smell.

Strain it through a cloth or a perforated tin into a bucket, and use it. Greywater flushes a working lavatory, damps down dust, washes the yard, rinses the latrine slab and waters fruit trees and ornamentals quite happily. Keep it off salad crops and off anything eaten raw, and pour laundry water — which is salty and alkaline — around trees rather than on a vegetable bed.

What you cannot use, soak away. A soak pit is a hole a metre or so deep filled with rubble, stones and coarse gravel, topped with a layer of sand and a lid of turf or boards, sited well away from any drinking source and downhill of it. Pour the strained water in and it disperses underground instead of standing in a stinking puddle. Empty and repack the top layer when it clogs, which it will.

Dishes take less water than anybody believes if you do them in three stages: scrape hard into the compost, wash in a small bowl of hot soapy water, then rinse from a jug or a garden sprayer held above the plate. Air-dry on a rack in the sun rather than on a tea towel that everyone has handled.

### Cleaning up after illness

This is the moment the household's health is decided, so do it properly and calmly. When somebody is vomiting or has diarrhoea, treat everything they touch as infectious for a couple of days after they feel better.

Clean first, disinfect second. Bleach is used up by dirt, so it is wasted on a surface you have not already washed. Wear gloves if you have them, open a window, and use disposable or dedicated cloths.

For surfaces soiled with vomit or faeces, a chlorine strength of 1,000 to 5,000 parts per million is the recommended range, which is roughly 20 to 100 millilitres of ordinary 5 per cent household bleach in one litre of water [src: CDC — Guideline for the Prevention and Control of Norovirus Gastroenteritis Outbreaks in Healthcare Settings, 2011]. Mix it fresh each time, because a bleach solution loses strength within a day. Leave the surface visibly wet for several minutes before wiping it off, since the contact time is what does the work.

Never mix bleach with ammonia, with vinegar or with any acidic descaler. The gas that comes off is dangerous in a small unventilated room. Bleach alone, in water, in a clearly marked container, and out of reach of children.

Soiled bedding and clothing go straight into a lidded bucket, not on the floor, and get washed at the hottest temperature the fabric will take, or soaked in a weak bleach solution first if hot water is scarce. Dry everything in full sunlight, which is a genuine disinfectant and free.

The ill person gets their own pail, their own cup, their own towel and their own bar of soap, and the strictest handwashing in the house — both theirs and yours. Rehydration matters far more than any other home treatment for diarrhoea, and the health chapter covers how to make and give it. Isolate the illness, not the person: a closed door with nobody visiting is worse than a chair by the window with a rule about cups.

> **Climate note (wet)** — Rain is the enemy of every pit ever dug. Site above the flood line, build a small earth bund uphill of the cubicle to turn surface water aside, roof the cubicle properly and raise the slab a hand's width above the surrounding ground. A pit that floods does not merely stop working; it lifts its contents into the yard and into the water table, and that is how one household's problem becomes the whole lane's. In prolonged wet, a raised bucket system beats a pit outright.

### Waste, rubbish and rats

Sort at source into four streams and the whole problem shrinks: compost, burnables, buryables, and keepables. Keepables are the jars, tins, wire, timber and containers that are suddenly worth money in a month with no shops — the fix-and-make chapter has plenty to say about that.

Food scraps go to compost, or to animals if you have them, and never into the latrine, which they fill without rotting. Keep the compost heap turned, covered and away from the kitchen door.

Burn what genuinely burns, in a fixed spot downwind, well away from buildings and stored fuel, with a bucket of water or sand to hand and never on a windy day. Do not burn plastics, foam, treated wood or aerosols where people are eating or children are playing; the smoke is unpleasant and worse than unpleasant.

Everything else goes into a dedicated rubbish pit, well away from the latrine and from water, with a soil cap added over each day's tipping. A covered pit does not attract rats or dogs; an open one becomes the centre of the village vermin problem inside a week. Keep a lidded bin at the house, empty it daily, and never let a bag sit outside overnight.

Rats and flies are not a hygiene failing to be embarrassed about; they are a signal that something is uncovered. When they appear, go looking for the lid that is missing rather than for a poison.

### Menstrual hygiene, nappies and dignity

Under stress these are the needs that get quietly dropped, and dropping them causes real illness and real misery.

Stock disposable supplies deeper than you think you need, and add reusable cloth pads or a menstrual cup, which need only water, soap and sunlight. Every toilet should have a lidded bin, a supply of water inside the cubicle rather than outside it, a hook, and a door that latches. Soaking buckets with lids let cloth be washed discreetly, and a private line in the sun dries it properly; where privacy is short, dry inside a pillowcase on the line. Burn or bury disposable products with the buryables, never into the pit.

Small children's faeces carry more infection than adults', not less, so nappies are a priority and not an afterthought. Scrape into the toilet, soak in a lidded bucket, wash hot, dry in sun. Wash the child's hands as well as your own — a crawling child's hands go everywhere, including into the mouths of others.

Anyone who cannot reach the latrine — elderly, injured, unwell — needs a commode or a lidded pail within a few steps of the bed, emptied without comment or fuss. How a household treats that person during a bad month is remembered long after the power comes back.

> **Climate note (cold)** — Nobody walks fifty metres to a freezing latrine at three in the morning, so plan for a covered pail in a porch or an unheated back room, with plenty of cover material. Keep a small can of hand-washing water indoors so it does not freeze, grit or board the path to the outside toilet, and remember that frozen ground cannot be dug: get the pit and a heap of dry cover material ready before the ground hardens. In deep cold, urine freezes into the pit and can build up as a column beneath the seat, so a separate urine container is the easier arrangement.

### Turning it back into soil

By the end of a month you will have full pails, and the honest answer for most households is a covered pit and time. But if the outage runs long, waste becomes a resource, and treating it properly is not difficult — it is just slow.

Use two composting bays built from pallets, sited away from water and roofed against rain. Fill one bay, always topping each addition with a generous layer of straw, sawdust or leaves, then cap it, seal it and start the second. The pile must be more carbon than nitrogen or it goes slimy and sour.

The safety rule is time and temperature. Composting that holds the whole mass above 55 °C for at least a week destroys the pathogens of concern; otherwise, store the sealed material for a year and longer in a cool climate before it is fit to touch [src: WHO — Guidelines for the Safe Use of Wastewater, Excreta and Greywater, Volume 4, 2006]. When in any doubt, treat it as raw: put finished compost around fruit trees, hedges and flowers rather than on anything eaten raw or growing at ground level, and wash hands and tools after handling it.

Urine is the easy half. Leaving a healthy person it carries little risk, and it is a strong nitrogen fertiliser. Dilute it about one part in ten with water, pour it on the soil rather than on the leaves, and rotate where you use it so salts do not build up in one bed. Collected separately, it also stops the pails smelling, which is reason enough on its own.

### When the mains and the sewer come back

Do not trust the system on its word. Flush the lavatory once and watch it drain completely before you flush a second time; if it is slow or gurgles, stop and wait. Run every tap and pour water down every unused floor drain and gully to refill the traps that have dried out, because a dry trap is an open pipe from the sewer into the house and the smell is the least of it.

Wash, disinfect and dry the bucket kit and store it complete, in one place, with the spare cover material. Do not dismantle the latrine for at least a fortnight: restored services fail again more often than anyone expects, and the second outage always arrives when you have just put the tools away.

Cap the pit properly when you do close it. Fill it with soil to well above ground level so it can settle, mark it, and plant a tree on it rather than a vegetable bed. It will be a good tree.

## Thrive Hacks

**The two-bucket rhythm.** One pail in use, one rinsed and drying in the sun. Swap at the same time each day and the job stops being a decision and becomes a habit, which is the only way unpleasant jobs survive week three.

**A wash bottle is better than paper.** A squeeze bottle or an old washing-up-liquid bottle with a fine hole in the cap gives you a controlled jet of clean water. It uses a fraction of a cupful, is far kinder on skin than any paper, and makes your paper supply last for months. Keep one bottle per person, marked, and wash hands properly afterwards regardless.

**Make the latrine somewhere people are content to be.** A lantern hook, a shelf, a bundle of dried mint or lavender hanging from a nail, a small mirror, a book on the shelf and a door that latches. It costs an hour and it is the difference between a facility people use and one they avoid, and avoidance is where disease starts.

**Wood ash is three tools in one.** Cover material for the pail, a mild scouring powder for pans and the slab, and an emergency hand-scrub when soap runs out. Keep a covered tub of it by the stove, sieved of charcoal lumps and kept dry.

**Sunlight is free disinfection.** A line in the sun does more for cloths, towels, nappies and boards than most of what is under the sink. Wash, then hang in the brightest place you have, and turn them once.

**The foot-operated door.** Screw a short batten low on the latrine door as a foot bar so it can be pushed and pulled without hands. Fit the same to the kitchen door. Freshly washed hands should not have to touch the handle the last person touched.

**Number your cloths.** Different colours or a stitched thread mark for latrine, kitchen, bodies and floors. When everything is washed in the same yard it takes about four days for the cloths to become one undifferentiated pile, and that pile is a transmission route.

**A wheelbarrow seat for the pit.** If the pit is any distance, carrying a full pail by hand is the job everybody dodges. A barrow with a milk crate screwed into it holds the pail upright, takes the weight and turns a horrible trip into a wheeled one.

**Keep a written sanitation log.** A sheet on the latrine door: date, who emptied, who scrubbed, cover material remaining. It takes five seconds, makes a slipping rota visible before anybody gets ill, and settles arguments about whose turn it is.
