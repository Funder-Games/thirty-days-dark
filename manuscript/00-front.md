# Thirty Days Dark

> A practical field manual for the first thirty days after the grid and the internet go down — written so that you can thrive, and not merely survive.

If the lights have just gone out, do not read this page. Turn to **The First Hour**, a few pages on, do the things on it, and come back when the household is fed and asleep.

This is a book about a month. Not a night, which is an inconvenience, and not a collapse, which is somebody else's genre. A month is long enough that the freezer empties, the taps stop, the phones die and the shops shut, and short enough that ordinary, sensible people get through it in good order if they know what to do in the first few days. Almost everything that goes badly wrong in that month is avoidable, and almost all of it is avoidable cheaply.

It is also a book about doing more than getting by. A household that has water, warmth, light and a rota has spare capacity, and spare capacity is what turns an emergency into a strange, memorable, oddly good few weeks: bread baked in a tin over a fire, a street that finally knows each other's names, children who learn to whittle. That is the *thrive* in these pages, and it is not decoration. Morale is a resource like water, and it runs out the same way.

## How to Use This Book

The book follows a time arc: the first hour, the first three days, the first week, and the three weeks after that. The skill chapters — water, power, food, heat, health, sanitation, comms, community, money, morale, repair — hang off that arc, so you can read it straight through as a story of a month, or dive at any chapter when the problem lands in your lap.

Every chapter is built the same way, on purpose. When you are tired and it is dark, you should not have to learn a new layout.

**Do This Now** is a single page at the front of every chapter, and it fits on that page so you can read it by torchlight without turning over. It is the version of the chapter for a person with ten minutes and a problem. If you only ever read the Do This Now cards, you will still be far better off than most.

**The Craft** is the how-to itself: the reasoning, the numbers, the methods and the mistakes. It is the part to read *before* you need it, ideally on a wet afternoon with a cup of tea.

**Thrive Hacks** are the clever bits — the improvisations, the free wins, the ideas that make a hard week easier or better. They are the most enjoyable part of the book and, in practice, the part people actually use.

**The Checklist** at the end of each chapter is designed to be photocopied, torn out or copied by hand onto a sheet on the wall. Tick boxes beat good intentions.

Four kinds of box appear throughout. A **Village Hack** is something that only works, or works far better, when several households do it together. A **Day-X Challenge** is a small rehearsal you can run today, because a plan you have never tested is a wish. **What I Wish I'd Stocked** is the honest hindsight list for that chapter — the cheap thing that turns out to matter. A **Climate note** flags where hot, wet or cold conditions change the advice.

There are three sensible ways to use the book.

1. **Before anything happens.** Read it through once, do two or three of the Day-X Challenges, and buy the short list of things you do not have. A weekend of work covers most of it.
2. **On the day.** Read The First Hour, then the Do This Now card of whichever chapter is biting. Nothing else. Come back to the craft when the immediate thing is handled.
3. **Through the month.** Take one chapter a day for the first fortnight, in the order your situation demands. Water first, always.

### On numbers, sources and judgement

Anything in this book that could hurt you if it were wrong — a treatment time, a temperature, a dose, a distance, a ratio — carries its source in small type beside it, like this [src: CDC — Making Water Safe in an Emergency, 2022]. Those figures come from public health and emergency bodies: the World Health Organization, the CDC, the Red Cross and Red Crescent, UNICEF, national health services, FEMA, the EPA and their equivalents. Where the honest answer is *it depends*, the book says so rather than inventing a number.

Everything else is craft: judgement, experience and the accumulated cleverness of people who have lived without services. Treat it as a good starting point and not as a rule. You know your house, your weather and your neighbours better than any book does, and the advice of your own local authority, in your own country, outranks these pages.

Two firm limits. This book covers first aid and home care only; it is not a substitute for a doctor, and where it says get medical help, get medical help. And it contains nothing about weapons, violence or tactics, because the evidence of real emergencies is overwhelming: the households that come through well are the ones that are useful to their neighbours, not the ones that are frightening to them.

### On place, and on copying this book

The setting is generally rural — a house with some land, a lane, a village within walking distance — because that is where self-reliance is most possible and help is furthest away. Most of it works just as well in a town, with the sharing done across a stairwell instead of a lane.

The book is deliberately not region-specific. Where climate changes the answer, a Climate note says how. Where the law or the local system changes the answer, you must fill that gap yourself: find out now, in writing, where your water comes from, who to call about a fallen cable, and where your nearest pharmacy, clinic and fire station are.

Finally: this book is free, and it is meant to be copied. It is published under a Creative Commons licence so that anyone may print it, photocopy it, translate it, improve it and hand it on. Print a copy. A manual that lives on a screen is a manual you cannot read on the evening you need it most, and the whole point of this one is that it works with a dead grid and a dark house.

## The First Hour

The power is off and you do not know for how long. Do these nine things, in this order, before anything else. Each names the chapter that goes deeper.

1. **Look outside, and write down the time.** One dark house is your fuse box; a dark street is a fault; a dark horizon is the grid. *(Day Zero)*
2. **Get a torch into every hand before dusk.** Head torches first, one lantern for the room you will all sit in. *(Day Zero)*
3. **Fill everything that holds water, then shut the stop tap.** Bath, pans, bottles, buckets, kettle. Pressure fades in hours. *(Water)*
4. **Shut the fridge and the freezer and tape a note on each.** They are clocks now, and every opening spends them. *(Food)*
5. **Unplug what a surge would kill, and leave one lamp on** as your telltale for when the power returns. *(Power)*
6. **Find the radio, fit fresh cells, and listen at the top of the hour.** Write down what was said, with the time. *(Comms)*
7. **Knock on anyone who cannot knock on you** — the old, the ill, the alone, anyone with medical equipment that needs power. *(Community)*
8. **Bring nothing that burns fuel indoors.** No generator, barbecue, charcoal tray, camping stove or patio heater, and not in a garage, porch or shed either [src: CDC — Carbon Monoxide Poisoning Prevention After an Emergency, 2021].
9. **Hold a household meeting before dark**, and give every person one job for tomorrow. *(Morale)*

> **The rule for tonight** — Nothing that burns fuel comes indoors, drinking water is treated, and nobody goes to bed without a torch beside them.
