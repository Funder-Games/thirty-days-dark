# Chapter 2 · Water

> Everything else in this book can wait a week. Water cannot wait a day.

[IMAGE: Cutaway of an ordinary house with the hidden water marked — loft tank, hot cylinder, pipework, toilet cisterns, water butt, kettle — arrows leading to a row of filled and labelled containers in a cool hallway]

## Do This Now

1. **Fill everything, now.** Bath, basins, buckets, pans, bottles, jugs, the kettle, the washing-machine drum, clean bins lined with a fresh bag. Mains pressure fades over hours, not days, so the first hour is worth more than the next ten.
2. **Then close the stop tap.** With your containers full, shut the incoming valve. It keeps whatever is already in your pipes and loft tank clean if the mains outside is later contaminated.
3. **Switch off the water heater** at its own switch or valve so it cannot fire on an empty cylinder.
4. **Label every container** with a marker or tape: DRINK, WASH, FLUSH. Mixing them up is how people get ill.
5. **Cover everything.** A lid, a plate, a bin bag and a bungee. Open water collects dust, insects and hands.
6. **Move drinking water somewhere cool and dark** — the coolest interior floor you have. Warmth and light are what spoil stored water.
7. **Write the day's household ration on the kitchen door** and the words: *drinking water is boiled or treated, always*.
8. **Put buckets under the downpipes** before you go to bed, whatever the forecast.
9. **Walk your water map** before dark: the nearest tap, stream, spring, pond, cattle trough, neighbour's tank. Note distances, not opinions.

Then sit down with a pencil and count what you actually have, in litres, on paper. Guessing is the enemy here.

## The Craft

Water is the fastest-moving problem in a blackout and the easiest one to get wrong in a way that hurts. Almost nobody dies of thirst in a grid-down month; people get ill from water they drank without thinking, or from hands they washed in water they should not have touched. So this chapter has two halves: getting enough, and making it safe. Both matter, and the second one is where the discipline goes.

### How much you actually need

Work from three numbers and you will never be badly wrong.

| Purpose | Per person, per day | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Drinking and food | 2.5–3 litres | [src: WHO — Technical Notes on Drinking-water, Sanitation and Hygiene in Emergencies, 2013] |
| Basic hygiene and cooking | 4–6 litres | [src: WHO — Technical Notes on Drinking-water, Sanitation and Hygiene in Emergencies, 2013] |
| Planning figure, all uses | 15 litres | [src: Sphere — Sphere Handbook: Minimum Standards in Humanitarian Response, 2018] |

Drinking goes up in heat and with hard work, and the hygiene line is the one people cut first and regret first. Hand-washing is not a luxury item on that list.

> **Climate note (hot)** — Heat doubles your drinking need and halves your patience. Move all water work to dawn and dusk, keep every container in shade, and wrap the drinking jerrycan in a wet towel: evaporation will take several degrees off it for free. Watch the children and the over-seventies closely, because both feel thirst late.

A family of four on the planning figure needs about sixty litres a day, which is three full jerrycans. That sounds a lot until you see how fast a bath and two buckets disappear. The short-term survival floor is lower — people manage for a few days on far less, at a real cost to hygiene and morale [src: WHO — Technical Notes on Drinking-water, Sanitation and Hygiene in Emergencies, 2013]. Plan on the higher figure and treat every litre under it as a day you have bought back.

Two habits beat any amount of storage. First, never let a container get below half without a plan to refill it. Second, drink on a schedule rather than on thirst, because thirst arrives late and a mildly dehydrated household makes bad decisions all day.

> **What I Wish I'd Stocked** — Two twenty-litre jerrycans with taps, a cheap folding sack barrow to move them, a dozen one-litre bottles for children and for taking water out to work, and a bottle of plain unscented bleach kept apart from the cleaning cupboard so nobody grabs the lemon-scented one by mistake.

### The water already in your house

Before you walk anywhere, inventory what the building is holding. Most houses contain a surprising reserve, and much of it is already clean.

| Where | Rough amount | Safe to drink? |
|---|---|---|
| Hot-water cylinder | The size of a small barrel | Yes, once cooled; draw from the drain cock at the bottom |
| Loft or header tank | A bath-full, in many older houses | Only if the tank is covered and clean; otherwise treat it |
| Pipework | A few jugs | Yes; open the lowest tap in the house to drain it |
| Toilet cistern (the tank, not the bowl) | A large jug each | Wash and flush only — never drink it, and never if a blue block or cleaner sits in it |
| Kettle, pans, vases, ice tray | A jug or two | Yes |
| Water butt, rain barrel | Often a bath-full or more | Treat it, always |

The bowl of a toilet is not water. Neither is anything from a radiator, a dishwasher, a softener's brine tank, or a swimming pool or hot tub, all of which carry chemicals you cannot boil out. Waterbeds are not drinking water either.

> **Village Hack** — Walk the lane on day one with a notebook and build the street's water map together: who has a well, who has a borehole with a hand pump, who has rain tanks, who has a stream at the bottom of the garden, who has livestock troughs. One map, copied by hand, pinned in four kitchens. The household that knows where the nearest hand pump is beats the household with the biggest bath.

### Making it clear before you make it safe

Every treatment method works better on clear water, and two of them barely work at all on cloudy water. Chlorine is used up by the muck it has to oxidise, and ultraviolet light cannot reach a microbe hiding behind a speck of silt. So clarify first, always, and treat second.

1. **Settle it.** Stand the water in a covered container for an hour or more and the grit drops out. Pour or siphon off the top, leaving the bottom behind.
2. **Strain it.** Four layers of clean cotton — a tea towel, a pillowcase, a T-shirt — over the mouth of a jug catches leaves, insects and most of the visible sediment. Rinse and re-boil the cloth when you can.
3. **Filter it.** A sand-and-charcoal column in a bucket with a hole in the bottom — coarse gravel, then fine gravel, then washed sand, then crushed charcoal from a clean wood fire — will take out fine silt, most of the colour and a lot of the smell. Slow is good; if it runs fast, it is not working.

[IMAGE: Exploded diagram of a bucket filter — gravel, sand and charcoal layers with a cloth pre-strainer over the top and a tap at the bottom]

Understand what that filter is and is not. A home-made sand filter makes water *clear*, not *safe*. Clarity is the first step, not the last one. The only home-made filter with a real claim on pathogens is a properly built and matured slow sand or biosand filter, and even then the conventional advice is to disinfect afterwards.

### The four ways to make water safe

| Method | Deals with | Does not deal with | Practical cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Boiling | Bacteria, viruses, protozoa including *Cryptosporidium* | Chemicals, salt, heavy metals | Fuel, and time to cool |
| Chlorine (household bleach) | Bacteria, most viruses | *Cryptosporidium*; struggles in cloudy water | A bottle lasts a household months |
| Filtration (manufactured, fine pore) | Protozoa, most bacteria | Viruses, unless the filter says otherwise | The cartridge is finite |
| Sunlight in clear bottles | Bacteria, viruses, protozoa | Chemicals; needs strong sun and clear water | Free, slow, weather-dependent |

**Boiling is the gold standard.** Bring the water to a rolling boil and hold it there for 1 minute; at altitudes above about 2,000 m, hold it for 3 minutes [src: CDC — Making Water Safe in an Emergency, 2022]. A rolling boil means big bubbles breaking the surface across the whole pan, not the first fizz at the edges. Let it cool covered, and do not sniff at it for reassurance — boiled water tastes flat because the dissolved air has gone, which is a sign it worked. Pour it between two clean jugs a few times if the flatness bothers people.

**Chlorine is the standard when fuel is short.** Use plain unscented liquid household bleach, sodium hypochlorite and nothing else — no thickeners, no scent, no colour-safe additives. For clear water: 2 drops of 5–6% bleach per litre, stirred well, then 30 minutes covered before you drink it. For water still cloudy after filtering: 4 drops per litre, and the same 30 minutes [src: EPA — Emergency Disinfection of Drinking Water, 2023]. The check is your nose: there should be a faint swimming-pool smell at the end. No smell means the dose was eaten by dirt, so dose again and wait again. The formal target behind that rule is a free chlorine residual of at least 0.5 mg per litre after 30 minutes of contact, in water that is not strongly alkaline [src: WHO — Guidelines for Drinking-water Quality, 4th edition, 2017].

Bleach loses strength on the shelf, faster in warmth and light. Buy small bottles, date them with a marker, keep them cool and dark, and replace them yearly. Chlorine tablets for drinking water are the tidier option if you can get them: read the packet, because tablet sizes differ, and the packet is the source of truth for the dose.

**Manufactured filters** earn their keep on stream and pond water. For the protozoa that cause the worst of the stomach misery, you want a filter rated at an absolute pore size of 1 micron or smaller, and most will not stop viruses on their own [src: CDC — Drinking Water Treatment Methods for Backcountry and Travel Use, 2020]. So the belt-and-braces routine for surface water is filter first, then boil or chlorinate. Keep the filter clean, backflush it as the maker says, and never let the dirty side touch the clean side — that is how a good filter poisons a good jug.

**Sunlight works and costs nothing.** Fill clear plastic bottles with clear water, lie them on their side on a bright surface — corrugated iron is ideal — and leave them in unbroken sun for about 6 hours; if the sky is overcast, give them 2 consecutive days [src: WHO — Combating Waterborne Disease at the Household Level, 2007]. It is the method to know about when the fuel has gone and the bleach has run out. It will not work on cloudy water, in a green bottle, or through a rain shower.

### Storing it so it keeps

Clean water goes bad in a dirty container faster than most people believe. The rules are dull and they work.

- **Food-grade containers only.** Old juice barrels, jerrycans, glass demijohns. Never a container that held fuel, solvent or garden chemicals, no matter how well you think you washed it.
- **Cool, dark, off the floor.** Warmth and daylight grow algae and bacteria; a concrete floor will taint plastic over time.
- **Full is better than half.** Air in the top of a drum is where the trouble starts.
- **One dipper, one job.** Never dip a cup or a hand into stored water. Fit a tap, or keep one dedicated ladle hanging beside the drum and nowhere else.
- **Rotate.** Drink from the oldest, refill it, put it at the back. Write the date on the side in marker.
- **Keep a treated reserve.** Water that has been chlorinated and sealed keeps for months. Untreated water in a warm room keeps for days.

> **Climate note (cold)** — Snow and ice are water, but eating them costs you body heat you cannot spare. Melt them first, and start the pan with a little liquid water in the bottom or the ice will scorch and taste of burnt tin. Stored water freezes and splits containers, so never fill above the shoulder, and keep the day's drinking water indoors.

### Rain: the system that pays you back

If you do one piece of building work in the first week, build a rain catchment. A roof is a large, free, gravity-fed collector, and rain arrives already soft and nearly clean.

One millimetre of rain falling on one square metre of roof gives you about one litre before losses, and a modest house roof is a lot of square metres. A single decent shower can fill a water butt. Do not take my word for it: catch one storm, measure what you got, and write the figure on the side of the tank in marker.

The four parts of a working system are a clean surface, a gutter, a first-flush diversion and a covered tank. The first flush matters: the beginning of the rain carries the roof's dust, droppings and lichen, so divert the first minute or two into the garden and only then swing the downpipe into the tank. A simple swinging elbow on the downpipe, or a bucket you empty and then reposition, does the job. Put a fine mesh over the tank inlet to keep leaves, insects and light out, and fit the tap a hand's width above the base so the silt stays put.

Treat rainwater before drinking it. Roofs are not clean, birds sit on them, and a thatched or asbestos-cement or freshly painted roof has its own problems — use those for washing water and drink from elsewhere.

> **Climate note (wet)** — A wet climate is a gift you have to catch. In monsoon or a long Atlantic winter your problem is never volume, it is contamination and storage: cover everything, divert the first dirty flush off the roof, and treat every drop that has touched a gutter.

> **Village Hack** — Pool the gutters, not just the water. One household with a workable roof, one length of spare downpipe and four neighbours with drums can set up a shared catchment in an afternoon, with a posted rota for who empties the first flush and who scrubs the tank. Shared infrastructure survives a month; shared enthusiasm does not.

### Streams, ponds, wells and springs

Rank your outdoor sources before you need them, because you will be carrying from one of them for weeks.

- **A spring** where water comes out of the ground is usually your best find. Take it at the source, not downstream, and protect the eye of it with stones so feet and animals stay out.
- **A hand-pumped well or borehole** is next, and is worth walking further for. Keep the surround clean and never let waste water pool back around the head.
- **A fast, clear stream** above any village, field drain or livestock is workable: take it from a riffle, not a still pool, and mid-depth rather than off the bottom.
- **A pond, canal or ditch** is a last resort. Assume it is contaminated, clarify it hard, then filter and boil.

Always look upstream before you fill. What is uphill of your water — a farmyard, a septic field, a graveyard, a road, a flooded cellar — matters far more than how pretty the water looks. Flood water is the worst of all: assume sewage, fuel and chemicals, and do not drink it however you treat it.

Carrying is the hidden tax. A twenty-litre can weighs about twenty kilograms, which is a serious load on a rough path, so use a wheelbarrow, a sack barrow, a bike with panniers or a child's wagon, and carry two smaller cans rather than one large one to keep your back straight and your hands balanced.

### What you must never drink, whatever you do to it

Treatment deals with living things. It does not deal with chemistry, and this is the one place where confidence gets people hurt.

- **Flood water.** Assume sewage, fuel, farm chemicals and whatever was in the shed uphill. Wash nothing in it, and keep children out of it.
- **Salt or brackish water.** Boiling concentrates the salt rather than removing it. Only distillation — catching the steam and cooling it — gives you fresh water from salt.
- **Water with a chemical smell, an oily sheen or a colour you cannot explain.** Walk away. A smell is a warning you should be glad you got.
- **A pond wearing a blue-green or paint-like bloom.** Boiling does not destroy the toxins some blooms carry, and can concentrate them [src: EPA — Harmful Algal Blooms and Drinking Water, 2022]. Neither will a jug filter. Find another source and keep livestock and dogs away too.
- **Water from a drum, tank or can that ever held fuel or chemicals**, however well it was rinsed.
- **Anything downhill of a dead animal, a latrine or a slurry store**, however clear it runs.

When you are not sure, and there is any alternative, take the alternative. When there is no alternative, clarify hard, filter, boil, and give it to the fittest adult in the house first.

### Rationing, and the household water ledger

A ration that lives in someone's head is not a ration. Keep a single sheet of paper on the kitchen wall with four columns — date, litres in, litres out, stock — and one pencil tied to it on a string. Update it when a container is filled or emptied, not later.

Then set the allocation out loud so nobody has to ask: so much for drinking, so much for cooking, so much for washing, and a fixed reserve nobody touches without the household agreeing. Name the reserve and mark it; an untouchable jerrycan with RESERVE on it in marker is worth more than a careful conversation.

Give the job to one person and a deputy. Water goes wrong when it is everybody's responsibility, which means nobody notices the drum is down to the silt at the bottom. And make the flush the first thing you cut: a single toilet flush can outweigh a person's entire drinking allowance for a day, which is why washing-up water and rain go down the pan and clean water never does.

### The hygiene half of the problem

Most grid-down illness is a water story told backwards: dirty hands, not dirty drink. So ring-fence some water for washing and never economise on it.

- A hand-washing station at the door of the house and beside the latrine, with soap, from day one.
- Soap, not disinfectant, and a full 20 seconds of rubbing before you rinse [src: CDC — When and How to Wash Your Hands, 2022]. Plain soap and doubtful water beats clean water and no soap.
- Separate cloths and separate basins for dishes, bodies and floors.
- Anyone with vomiting or diarrhoea gets their own cup, their own basin and the strictest hand-washing in the house. Rehydration matters more than any other treatment in a home setting [src: WHO — The Treatment of Diarrhoea: A Manual for Physicians and Other Senior Health Workers, 2005].
- Boil water for anyone unwell, even if the household is drinking chlorinated water happily.

### When the mains comes back

Do not celebrate by filling a glass. The first water down a restarted main is the dirtiest water that pipe will ever carry: rust, scale and whatever got in while the pressure was off.

Run the cold tap nearest the incoming main — the kitchen, usually — until the water runs clear and cold and stops smelling of pipe. Run it into buckets for the garden rather than down the drain. Work outward through the rest of the taps, then flush the toilets a few times, then run the hot side, then finally the appliances. Take the aerators off the tap ends, rinse the grit out of them, and put them back.

Assume a boil-water rule is in force until somebody in authority says otherwise, and keep boiling for drinking and for brushing teeth in the meantime. Throw out ice made during the outage. Keep your stored water and your treatment kit exactly as they are for a fortnight after the taps return, because restored services fail again more often than people expect.

> **Day-X Challenge** — Day three: turn the tap off at the stop cock for twenty-four hours and live off what you have stored and carried. Weigh the cans before and after. Most households discover their real consumption is double their estimate, and that the flush is the biggest thief in the building.

## Thrive Hacks

**The tippy-tap.** Hang a bottle or small can with a nail hole near the neck from a stick frame, tie a cord from the bottle to a foot pedal, and hang the soap on a string beside it. Step, and a thin stream runs; lift your foot and it stops. Hands never touch the container, and a two-litre bottle gives a whole family a day of proper washing. It is the single highest-value thing you can build in an hour.

**Gravity is free plumbing.** Anything you can get above head height gives you pressure. A drum on a sturdy platform, a bucket on a stepladder, a bag in the loft with a hose out of it: suddenly you can rinse dishes, fill a bottle one-handed and shower properly. Get the drinking drum high and up on a plinth first, and fit a tap to it.

**The bag shower.** A dark bin bag or black jerrycan of water left in the sun all afternoon, hung from a branch with a hose and a pinch clip, is a warm shower at dusk. Morale returns with astonishing speed when people are clean. Test the temperature with the back of your hand before children get in.

**A garden sprayer is a pressure washer.** A pump-up pesticide sprayer, bought new and never used for chemicals, gives you a controlled jet for washing hands, rinsing wounds, cleaning dishes with almost no water, and washing a child's face without a fight.

**Clear bottles are a solar kettle.** Keep a permanent line of filled clear bottles on the sunniest metal or pale surface you own, with a cardboard label on each showing the day it went out. A rolling batch means you always have sun-treated water coming, and it costs nothing but bottles.

**Condensation catches water from nothing.** A clear sheet over a leafy shrub, or a sheet over a hole in damp ground with a stone in the middle and a cup beneath, gives you a cup or two a day of distilled water — never enough to live on, but enough to matter, and it works on brackish or dirty ground water because only the vapour travels.

**Cook in the water you drink.** Steam vegetables rather than boiling them dry, keep the cooking water for soup, and boil the pasta in exactly the water you need. Every pan you throw away is a pan somebody carried.

**Ice is storage.** While a freezer still has any cold in it, fill the empty space with bottles of water. They hold the cold for the food, and they are your cleanest reserve when they melt. Leave a finger of air at the top of each.

**Flavour is a tool, not a luxury.** Mint, lemon balm, a slice of any citrus, a spoon of squash, or a pinch of salt and sugar makes flat boiled water drinkable, and children who like the taste actually drink. A sprig of mint in the jug is worth an argument avoided.
