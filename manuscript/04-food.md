# Chapter 4 · Food

> Nobody starves in the first month. People get ill from food they should have thrown away, and miserable from meals nobody planned.

[IMAGE: Kitchen table laid out as a triage line — the fridge's contents on the left to be eaten first, the freezer block wrapped in blankets in the middle, sealed jars and dry goods stacked on the right, with a paper ledger and a pencil in the foreground]

## Do This Now

1. **Keep the fridge and freezer shut.** Unopened, a fridge holds food safely for about 4 hours, a full freezer for about 48 hours, and a half-full freezer for about 24 [src: USDA — Food Safety During a Power Outage, 2023].
2. **Pack the freezer tight** before it warms: everything into one dense block in the middle, gaps filled with bottles of water, blankets over the lid. Crowded food keeps itself cold.
3. **Cook the fridge tonight.** Meat, fish, milk and leftovers into one big pot, eaten hot, the rest kept cool and finished tomorrow.
4. **Eat in this order** for the next few days: fridge, then freezer, then fresh from the garden, then the dry store. The tins are last, not first.
5. **Take all cooking outside.** Never burn charcoal, a barbecue or a camping stove indoors or in a porch [src: CDC — Carbon Monoxide Poisoning Prevention, 2021].
6. **Count the dry store in days, not items,** on one sheet of paper: how many days of meals for how many people.
7. **Say the ration out loud** tonight, so no one has to ask tomorrow.
8. **Pest-proof the store.** Everything loose into hard containers with lids, off the floor.
9. **Write the rule on the kitchen door:** *if it was warm all day, it is compost, not dinner.*

## The Craft

Food is the problem people worry about first and lose least sleep over in the end. A month is not a famine. What a month without power actually does is take away your cold store, your shops, your oven and your habits all in one afternoon, and leave you cooking unfamiliar food on unfamiliar fuel for people who are already tired. Handle that well and you will eat better in week four than in week one.

Three jobs, in order: save what is about to spoil, stretch what keeps, and keep the kitchen clean enough that nobody in the house gets ill. The third one is the one that hurts if you get it wrong.

### The cold chain you have just lost

Cold is not storage, it is a clock. The moment the power goes, that clock starts, and the only way to slow it is to leave the doors closed.

| Where | How long it stays safe | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Fridge, unopened | About 4 hours | [src: USDA — Food Safety During a Power Outage, 2023] |
| Full freezer, unopened | About 48 hours | [src: USDA — Food Safety During a Power Outage, 2023] |
| Half-full freezer, unopened | About 24 hours | [src: USDA — Food Safety During a Power Outage, 2023] |

Bacteria multiply fastest in the band between about 4 °C and 60 °C, which is why perishable food held above 4 °C for more than 2 hours should be thrown out rather than sniffed at [src: USDA — Danger Zone (40 °F – 140 °F), 2020]. Smell and taste are not tests. Plenty of food that will make a household very ill smells of nothing at all, and the temptation to save an expensive piece of meat is exactly how a kitchen puts four people to bed.

So buy yourself a fact instead of an argument. A cheap fridge thermometer left in the cool box, and a probe thermometer for the pot, are the two most useful pieces of kit in this chapter. The safe fridge target is at or below 4 °C and the freezer at or below −18 °C [src: FDA — Refrigerator Thermometers: Cold Facts about Food Safety, 2023]. Once you can read the number, decisions stop being about hope.

When the freezer finally gives up, food that still holds ice crystals, or that is still at or below 4 °C, can be refrozen or cooked; food that has gone warm and soft goes [src: USDA — Food Safety During a Power Outage, 2023]. Throw out anything that has touched flood water, and any tin that is bulging, badly dented at the seam, rusted through or leaking [src: FDA — Food Safety for Consumers After a Flood, 2023].

> **What I Wish I'd Stocked** — A probe thermometer and a spare battery for it. A dozen wide-mouth jars with sound lids. A big stockpot with a tight lid and a bag of rice, lentils, oil, salt, sugar, tea and powdered milk kept as one untouched block at the back, dated in marker. Nothing exotic: a month is not the time to learn to like something new.

### Cook down, then eat down

Write the order on paper on day one and it settles a dozen small arguments. Eat the fridge, then the freezer, then whatever is ripe outside, then the dry store, then the tins. The tins are your reserve precisely because they need no cold and no argument.

The big cook-down on the first evening is the single highest-value hour in the week. Everything perishable goes into one pot — the mince, the sausages, the chicken, the half-bag of vegetables, the milk that will not last — and comes out as a stew, a curry or a soup that feeds the household twice. Cooked food keeps better than raw, reheats fast, and is far less trouble to store safely.

Bring anything you cook up to a real temperature, not a warm one. Poultry to 74 °C, minced meat to 71 °C, whole cuts of beef, pork or lamb to 63 °C with a three-minute rest, and leftovers reheated right through to 74 °C [src: USDA — Safe Minimum Internal Temperature Chart, 2023]. Cook once, cool fast, eat within a day unless you have a working cold store, and never keep a pot warm on a dying stove for hours, which is the most reliable way to grow something unpleasant in a family dinner.

> **Day-X Challenge** — Day four: cook a full household meal on one burner, or on one small fire, using only what is in the cupboard and nothing from the fridge. Time it, weigh the fuel you used, and write both numbers on the back of the ledger. That figure is your real fuel budget for the month.

### The dry store, and what actually keeps

Once the cold food is gone, your household runs on the dry store, so it is worth knowing which parts of it are patient and which are quietly dying in the cupboard.

| Kept cool, dark and airtight | Roughly how patient | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| White rice, pasta, sugar, salt, honey | Years, effectively | Damp and weevils, not age |
| Dried beans, lentils, split peas | Years, but they cook slower as they age | Very old beans that never soften |
| Flour, oats, wholegrains, nuts, seeds | Months | Rancid, crayon-like smell from the oils |
| Cooking oil, unopened | Months | Warmth and daylight, which age it fast |
| Milk powder, tinned fish and meat, tinned fruit | Long, unopened | Rust, dents at the seam, bulging |
| Yeast, baking powder, spices | Loses strength first, not safety | Flat bread and flavourless food |

Four rules cover the lot. Cool, dark and dry beats everything else, so the coolest interior floor wins over the warm cupboard above the stove. Air and damp are the enemies, so decant opened bags into hard containers with real lids. Fat goes off before starch does, so buy oil and wholemeal flour small and use them in order. And rotate: oldest at the front, the date on the side in marker, and one person who knows what is in there.

Keep the store in one place, on shelves, off the floor, and go and look at it once a week with a torch. Mice, moths and weevils are a bigger threat to a month's food than hunger is, and they are quiet until they are not. Anything that arrives in a paper bag is already a mouse's invitation.

> **Climate note (wet)** — Damp spoils a dry store faster than heat does. Get everything into airtight containers with a handful of dry rice or a sachet of silica in the bottom, keep the store off cold outside walls where condensation forms, and dry your firewood and your food racks under cover before you need them. Check for mould weekly: a fuzzy patch on one loaf or one jar means the whole shelf gets inspected, and mouldy soft food goes out rather than being cut back.

> **Village Hack** — Run a swap shelf. One table in a porch or a shed where households put what they have too much of and take what they lack — a glut of courgettes for a bag of flour, tea for candles, a jar of jam for eggs. No money, no ledger, no argument about worth, and it turns five narrow larders into one broad one within a week.

### Rationing without misery

A ration that lives in someone's head is not a ration. Sit down with the dry store, divide it by the number of mouths, and turn it into days — the only unit that matters. For planning purposes, humanitarian agencies work on roughly 2,100 kcal per person per day, with more for anyone pregnant, feeding a baby, ill or doing heavy physical work [src: Sphere — Sphere Handbook: Minimum Standards in Humanitarian Response, 2018].

Then ration sideways rather than downward. Cut variety and portion creep before you cut meals, because three small predictable meals hold a household together far better than two large ones and a long hungry evening. Keep the shape of the day: something hot in the morning, something hot at night, and a pot of tea in between that costs almost nothing and buys an hour of goodwill.

- Keep one visible **reserve** nobody touches without the household agreeing, marked as such.
- Give the store to **one person and a deputy**, the same as the water.
- Feed the **cooks and the carriers** properly; the household that starves its workers stops working.
- Keep a **treat box** and open it on a bad day. Morale is a nutrient in a dark month.

> **Village Hack** — Pool the pots, not just the food. One fire, one big pan, five households taking turns to cook and to gather fuel, is a fraction of the fuel, the water and the washing-up of five kitchens working alone. Post a rota on a door, keep each family's ingredients in its own labelled bag, and let whoever cooks eat first. A street kitchen also makes it obvious, without anybody having to admit it, which house has run out.

### Cooking on very little fuel

Most of the food in your cupboard is dried and needs long, wet, boring heat, which is exactly the heat that costs fuel. Three tricks cut that cost hard.

**Soak first.** Beans, lentils, grains and dried peas soaked overnight in clean water cook in a fraction of the time. Soaking is free; boiling is not.

**Retain the heat.** Bring the pot to a rolling boil for a few minutes, lid on, then bury it in a box packed with blankets, straw, crumpled paper or hay. It carries on cooking for hours on the heat it already has. A hay box will do your beans, your rice, your porridge and your stew, and it works while you are out.

**Cut it small and use a lid.** Smaller pieces and a tight lid together can halve your cooking time. A pan boiling its heart out with the lid off is burning fuel to make steam.

Choose the fire for the job: twigs and a small efficient stove for a fast boil, embers for long simmering, a solar cooker on a bright day for anything you can leave alone. Whatever the stove, it lives outdoors or in a fully open outbuilding, and the fuel store lives well away from it. Carbon monoxide from cooking indoors kills people who did everything else right [src: CDC — Carbon Monoxide Poisoning Prevention, 2021].

> **Climate note (hot)** — In heat, food turns in hours. Cook in the cool of the morning for the day, keep nothing cooked overnight unless it is in a cold store, and take the kitchen outside so the house does not become an oven. A damp cloth over a covered bowl, in shade and in a draught, is a poor man's fridge and better than a warm cupboard.

### Keeping food without cold

Every preserving method does the same thing by a different route: it takes away what spoilage needs — water, air, warmth or a friendly pH.

**Dry it.** The oldest and cheapest method, and the best fit for a dark month. Thin slices of apple, pear, tomato, mushroom, onion and green herbs will dry on racks in moving air, in the sun under a mesh, or over the residual warmth of a stove. Dry until brittle or leathery with no moisture left in the thickest part, then store airtight in the dark. Meat is a different matter: heat it right through before drying it, not after, because a low-temperature dryer alone does not make raw meat safe [src: USDA — Jerky and Food Safety, 2023].

**Salt it.** Salt and sugar both pull water out of food and out of anything trying to live in it. Dry-salted fish and pork, salt-cured beans, jams and fruit in honey all keep for months at room temperature, and the tradition is worth reading up on properly before you need it.

**Ferment it.** The most forgiving preservation there is, and the one that adds vitamins rather than losing them. Shred cabbage, weigh it, mix in roughly 2 per cent salt by that weight, pack it under its own brine so nothing sits in the air, and keep it cool and covered until it smells pleasantly sour [src: USDA — Complete Guide to Home Canning, 2015]. The same method turns carrots, turnips, beans and cucumbers into food that keeps. If it grows fuzzy mould, smells rotten rather than sour, or slimes, it goes out.

**Keep it cool.** A hole in the shaded north side of the garden, lined with a bin and a lid; a shelf on the coldest interior floor; a wet cloth over a cage in a draught; a stream with a weighted, sealed jar in it. None of these is a fridge, but the difference between a warm larder and a cool one is days of food.

### Bottling, and the one rule about botulism

Home canning is the one part of this chapter where improvisation is genuinely dangerous. Acid foods — fruit, jam, pickles, chutneys, tomatoes with added acid — can be processed in a boiling-water bath. Low-acid foods, which means vegetables, beans, meat, fish, soups and stews, cannot be made safe that way and need a pressure canner, because the bacterium that causes botulism survives boiling in a sealed low-acid jar [src: USDA — Complete Guide to Home Canning, 2015].

So follow a tested recipe and its own times and pressures exactly. Do not scale a recipe up, thicken it, or invent one. If you have no pressure canner, do not bottle low-acid food at all; dry it, ferment it, salt it or eat it.

Then treat every home-bottled jar with respect when you open it. Never taste food to decide whether it is safe. Anything with a bulging lid, a leaking seal, a spurt of liquid on opening, a bad smell or a cloudy brine goes out unopened where nothing can get at it, and the surfaces get washed. Boiling low-acid home-canned food for 10 minutes before it is eaten, with extra time at altitude, is the standing precaution [src: USDA — Complete Guide to Home Canning, 2015].

### Food that arrives while you wait

Nothing you plant in week one feeds you in week one, but two things grow fast enough to matter.

**Sprouts.** Any whole dried pulse or seed from the cupboard — mung beans, lentils, chickpeas, wheat, sunflower — rinsed twice a day in a jar with a cloth over the top, gives you fresh green food in days, in the dark, with no soil and almost no water. It is the cheapest vitamin C in the house, and it is what stops a tinned diet feeling like one. Rinse properly, keep the jar draining rather than standing in water, and eat them cooked if anyone in the house is unwell.

**Fast greens.** Radish, rocket, mustard, spinach and cut-and-come-again salad in a tray, a gutter or a bucket give leaves within weeks. Plant something every single week, even in month one, because the person planting in week two is the person eating in week five.

And look at what is already growing: the herbs in the pot by the door, the fruit nobody picks off the tree at the end of the lane, the nettles behind the shed, the eggs from the neighbour's hens. Eggs are the quiet hero of a dark month — they arrive daily, they need no cold store for a while if they are unwashed and kept cool, and they turn a plate of starch into a meal.

> **Climate note (cold)** — Cold is a free fridge and a hard kitchen. Use an unheated outbuilding, a cellar or a snowbank as your cold store, but keep food off the ground and in hard containers, because everything hungry is looking for it too. Watch out for food freezing and splitting jars, and remember that cooking and keeping warm now compete for the same fuel, so a hay box earns its keep twice over.

### Foraging, and the rule that keeps you alive

Wild food is a good supplement and a bad staple. It is worth learning, worth walking for, and worth being strict about.

**The rule, and it does not bend: if you are not completely certain what a plant or fungus is, you do not eat it.** Not a nibble, not cooked, not *it looked like the one in the book*. There is no reliable field test for edibility; the old routine of rubbing a leaf on your lip and waiting does not detect the things that damage a liver, and the deadliest mushrooms are the ones that taste pleasant and make you ill two days later, long after the damage is done. Eat only what you can name with certainty, from a plant you have seen identified before, in a place you would be happy to eat soil from.

Work in this order, easiest and safest first:

- **Water and the coast:** shellfish only where you know the water is clean and legal to gather from, which after a flood or a sewage failure means nowhere.
- **Fruit and nuts:** brambles, elder, rosehips, sloes, hazel, sweet chestnut, crab apples. Hard to confuse, and the best calories in the hedge.
- **Greens:** nettle tops, dandelion, chickweed, sorrel, wild garlic. Cook nettles and always look at where they are growing, not just what they are.
- **Roots and fungi:** last and most cautious. Umbellifers — the carrot family, with flat flower heads — include both dinner and some of the most poisonous plants in the region, and are the classic fatal confusion. Leave the whole family alone unless a knowledgeable person has it in their hand.

Never gather beside a busy road, a sprayed field, a slurry store or a flood line, and never strip a patch; take a little from many places so it is still there next month. Introduce one new wild food at a time, in a small amount, so that if it disagrees with somebody you know what did it.

### Hands, water and the table

Most illness in a grid-down month arrives through the kitchen. The defences are dull and they work: wash hands with soap before cooking and before eating, keep raw and cooked food apart and on separate boards, wash fruit and vegetables in treated water, keep everything covered, and wash up in the hottest water you can make with a final rinse in clean water. Boil water for drinking and for anything eaten raw [src: WHO — Five Keys to Safer Food Manual, 2006].

Give one person the job of watching the kitchen the way one person watches the water. Keep a bowl of soapy water by the door, keep the compost and the scraps well away from where food is prepared, and get the food waste out of the house daily, because rats arrive within days of a kitchen going careless.

### Feeding the people who cannot wait

Small children, older people and anyone unwell are the reason the ration is not equal. Babies come first. If a baby is breastfed, protect the mother's food and water before anyone else's; if a baby is formula fed, make each feed fresh with water that has been boiled and left to cool for no more than 30 minutes, so it is still hot enough to kill anything in the powder [src: NHS — How to make up baby formula, 2023]. Never dilute a feed to stretch the tin, and never hold a made-up feed warm for hours.

For anyone with vomiting or diarrhoea, fluid matters far more than food: give oral rehydration solution in small frequent sips and keep offering food as soon as they will take it [src: WHO — The Treatment of Diarrhoea: A Manual for Physicians and Other Senior Health Workers, 2005]. Older people eat less than they need, feel thirst late, and will quietly go without to leave more for the children, so serve them rather than letting them serve themselves, and put a spoon of oil or milk powder into what they do eat.

### When the shops and the power come back

Do not open the freezer and start bargaining with it. Work through it with the thermometer and the rules above, and let the borderline items go — a week's food is cheaper than a week of a household being ill. Wash down the fridge, the freezer and the surfaces, throw out anything absorbent that held spoiled food, and keep the dry store, the jars and the outdoor stove exactly as they are for a fortnight, because restored services fail again more often than people expect.

Then sit down and write what you actually ate, what ran out first, and what nobody touched. That single page is worth more than any list in any book, because it is about your household.

## Thrive Hacks

**The hay box is a second cook.** A cardboard box, a bin bag, an old duvet and a cushion make a cooker that needs no fuel and no attention. Boil hard for a few minutes, bury the pot, walk away, eat in the evening. Beans, rice, porridge, stew, even a pudding. One hour of fire becomes six hours of cooking.

**The sprout jar on the windowsill.** Keep a rolling row of three jars — one started today, one halfway, one ready — and you always have something fresh and crunchy. It costs a spoonful of dried beans and thirty seconds of rinsing twice a day, and it transforms a month of stodge.

**Bake on the stove, not in an oven.** A heavy lidded pan with a trivet or a bed of sand in the bottom bakes flatbread, soda bread and scones perfectly well over a small fire. Flatbread needs no rising, no tin and barely any fuel, and hot bread on a bad day does something no ration can.

**One pot, three dinners.** Cook a big pot of beans or grain plain, then take a third out each day and change its character with what you have: fried onion and spice today, stewed tomato tomorrow, milk and sugar as a pudding after that. Same fuel, three meals, no boredom.

**Grow in the gutter.** Guttering, drainpipe, buckets, bags of soil and window boxes all grow salad and radish faster than beds do, and you can put them where the light is and carry them out of the frost. Plant something every week without fail.

**The cool cupboard.** Hang a wet hessian sack or towel over a shelf unit in a draught, keep its bottom edge in a tray of water, and stand the milk, butter and cooked food inside it. Evaporation buys you real degrees for free, and the water it uses is the water you were going to put on the garden anyway.

**Label and date everything, even leftovers.** Masking tape and a marker end every argument about whether something is still all right, and they make the ledger honest. What is not dated gets eaten first or thrown first.

**Trade cooked, not raw.** A pot of soup, a loaf, a jar of sauerkraut or an afternoon of your fire's spare heat is worth far more to a neighbour than a cup of flour, and it builds the kind of street where nobody is quietly going hungry.

**Keep a recipe card box of no-power meals** that your household actually likes, written out by hand, with the fuel each one needs in the corner. Somebody tired at dusk should not have to invent dinner.
