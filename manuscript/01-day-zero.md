# Chapter 1 · Day Zero

> The first hour sets the tone of the first month. Spend it on light, people, water and a closed fridge door.

[IMAGE: A dark street at dusk seen from a front doorstep — one window lit by a lantern, a neighbour crossing the road with a torch, a paper note going into a letterbox]

## Do This Now

1. **Look outside before you touch anything.** One dark house is your fuse box. A dark street is a local fault. A dark horizon, with no glow over the town, is the grid.
2. **Write down the time it went off.** Put the sheet on the kitchen door. Everything else dates from that line.
3. **Get light into every hand before dusk.** A torch each, a lantern per room, and spare batteries out of the drawer and onto the table.
4. **Shut the fridge and freezer and leave them shut.** Tape a note on each door. They are now clocks, not cupboards.
5. **Fill your water containers** while the pressure lasts, then close the stop tap. Chapter 2 is the whole story; the first hour is the part you cannot get back.
6. **Switch off and unplug** the boiler, oven, computers, chargers and anything with a motor. Leave one lamp on as your telltale.
7. **Find the radio**, put fresh batteries in it, and listen at the top of the hour.
8. **Knock on the neighbours who cannot knock on you** — anyone old, ill, alone, or running something medical off the mains.
9. **Nothing that burns fuel comes indoors.** No generator, no barbecue, no camping stove, no patio heater. Not in the garage or porch either.
10. **Hold a household meeting before dark** and give everyone one job for tomorrow.

## The Craft

Day zero is not the day you solve anything. It is the day you avoid the three mistakes that cost households the most: burning something indoors, opening the freezer, and letting the first evening pass without anyone deciding who does what. Everything in this chapter is cheap, fast and reversible. The clever work starts tomorrow.

### The first ten minutes: how far does the dark go?

Before you do anything skilful, find out what has actually happened. Step outside and look properly. Are your neighbours' windows lit? Is there a glow over the nearest town? Are the streetlights out, and the traffic lights, and the little red standby dots in every house on the lane? A single dark house is a tripped breaker or a blown fuse and you can fix it in a minute. A dark street is a fault someone is already driving towards. A dark horizon is a different kind of evening.

Check your own consumer unit first, in that spirit: if one switch has tripped, reset it once. If it trips again, leave it and unplug things instead. If your own board looks normal and the street is dark, stop poking at it and put the torch down.

Then check the phone. A mobile mast has its own battery, which is good for hours rather than days, so a working signal now says nothing about tonight. Send your short messages first — *we are fine, power is out here, do not worry* — because a text will squeeze through a congested network long after a call will not. Then put the phone in low-power mode and stop refreshing things.

Two questions matter and only one of them is answerable. How wide is it? You can find that out in ten minutes with your eyes and a radio. How long will it last? Nobody knows on day zero, and the honest answer will not arrive for days. So use the planner's trick: assume it is long, act calmly today, and be delighted to be wrong tomorrow. A household that prepares for a month and gets its power back on Thursday has lost nothing but one evening of effort. A household that assumes Thursday and is wrong has lost a freezer, a week of sleep and its temper.

> **Village Hack** — Before it is fully dark, walk the lane with a pencil and a sheet of paper and write down who is in, who is out, who has a wood stove, who has bottled gas, who has a working radio, who has a well, and who is going to need help by Friday. One sheet, copied by hand into four kitchens, is the most valuable object on the street by day three. It also means nobody spends the week wondering whether the old chap at the end is all right.

### What to switch off, and what to leave on

Power does not come back politely. It returns as a surge, sometimes twice in a minute, and it can take out the boiler and the freezer that survived the whole outage. So spend ten minutes now unplugging, and you will not spend a fortnight regretting it.

| Thing | Do this | Because |
|---|---|---|
| Computers, TV, router | Unplug at the wall | A surge kills the board, not the fuse |
| Boiler, heat pump, immersion | Switch off at its own switch | It must not fire dry or restart unattended |
| Oven, hob, kettle, iron | Switch off and check nothing was left on | It will come back on with nobody in the room |
| Washing machine, dishwasher | Switch off, and empty it when you can | A mid-cycle restart floods a kitchen |
| Electric vehicle charger | Switch off at its isolator | It will pull hard the instant supply returns |
| One lamp, somewhere visible | Leave it switched on | Your telltale: it lights up when the power is back |

Leave that single lamp on. It is the only reliable way to know the supply has returned at three in the morning, and a hall lamp is better than a bedroom one because you will see it without getting up.

Gas deserves its own minute. If you smell gas at any point, do not touch a switch or a flame: open the windows, turn the supply off at the meter, get everyone out, and do not go back in until it is clear. Otherwise leave the gas on — a gas hob you can light with a match is the single most useful surviving appliance in most houses — but remember that a modern boiler will not run without electricity, so hot water is now a pan on the hob, not a tap.

Outside your own walls, do nothing at all. Assume every fallen cable is live, keep children and animals well away from it, and report it if you have any way to. A cable across a lane is not a job for a householder with a torch.

### What to grab in the first hour

Work in this order, because it is the order in which things get harder to do.

**Light, before dusk.** One torch per person, a lantern for each room you will actually use, the battery drawer emptied onto the table so you know what you really have, and every rechargeable light on the windowsill in the morning. Doing this at four in the afternoon takes ten minutes. Doing it at nine in the evening takes an hour and a bruised shin.

**Water, while the pressure lasts.** Fill the bath, the pans, the bottles, the kettle and the buckets, then shut the stop tap. Plan on around 3 litres per person per day for drinking alone, and more in heat or hard work [src: WHO — Technical Notes on Drinking-water, Sanitation and Hygiene in Emergencies, 2013]. Chapter 2 tells you how to make it safe and how to make it last; today just get it into containers with lids.

**Warmth, information, medicine, money, documents.** Get the blankets and the warm layers out of the loft before you need them. Put the radio and its batteries on the kitchen table. Find every medicine in the house and put it in one box, including the ones in handbags and bedside drawers. Get the cash you have into small notes in one place, because card machines and cash points are electrical objects. Put passports, insurance papers and the deeds in one waterproof bag by the door — not because you are leaving, but because you will never find them by torchlight.

> **What I Wish I'd Stocked** — A head torch for every person, because a hand torch means one hand for everything else. A wind-up or battery radio with a spare set of cells taped to it. A box of rechargeable cells and a small solar panel to fill them. A manual tin opener. A full spare gas cylinder. A hundred pounds in fives and tens. A paper list of every phone number that matters, because your contacts live in a device with a battery.

### The cold chain: your fridge and freezer are clocks

From the moment the power goes, your appliances are running down a fixed amount of stored cold, and every door opening spends some of it. The figures are worth knowing exactly.

| Appliance | Holds food safely for | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Refrigerator, kept closed | About 4 hours | [src: USDA — Keeping Food Safe During an Emergency, 2022] |
| Freezer, full, kept closed | About 48 hours | [src: USDA — Keeping Food Safe During an Emergency, 2022] |
| Freezer, half full, kept closed | About 24 hours | [src: USDA — Keeping Food Safe During an Emergency, 2022] |

So the rule is simple and unpopular: the doors stay shut. Decide what you want out, write it on the note taped to the door, and open it once. A fridge opened six times in an evening is a cupboard at room temperature by morning.

Three moves buy you time. Move anything from the fridge that you are not eating today into the freezer, where the cold is. Fill the freezer's empty air with bottles or bags of water, because a full freezer holds its cold far longer than a half-empty one. And find the coldest place in the house — a north-facing stone floor, a cellar, an unheated porch — and make it your cold larder for butter, cheese and anything opened.

When the doors do come open, judge food by temperature and time, not by smell or hope. Throw out perishable food — meat, fish, poultry, eggs, milk, soft cheese, leftovers, anything cooked — that has been above 4 °C for more than two hours [src: USDA — Keeping Food Safe During an Emergency, 2022]. Food in the freezer that still holds ice crystals, or is still at or below 4 °C, can be refrozen or cooked [src: USDA — Keeping Food Safe During an Emergency, 2022]. When you are not sure, throw it out: a lost chicken is an annoyance, and a household with food poisoning in a grid-down week is a serious problem with no easy exit.

Then be sensible about the rest. Tonight you eat the thing that will spoil first, not the thing you fancy. That usually means a big cooked meal of everything defrosting, which is exactly the meal to share with a neighbour who has no way to cook. Day zero dinner is the best social investment of the whole month.

> **Climate note (hot)** — Heat runs the cold clock faster and shortens every figure above. Move the freezer into the coolest room if you can, shade it from any window, and throw a blanket over the top and sides while leaving the vents and the back clear. Eat the fridge out completely on the first day rather than the second, and do all your cooking at dawn or after dusk so you are not heating the house you are trying to keep cool.

### The one that kills: engines, flames and air

Almost every death in a blackout is either a fire or carbon monoxide, and both come from someone cold, tired and improvising after dark. This is the one section of the book to read twice.

Carbon monoxide has no colour and no smell, and it comes from anything burning fuel: a generator, a petrol pump, a barbecue, a charcoal tray, a camping stove, a patio heater, a car engine. It does not need to be in the room with you. It seeps through a wall from a garage, up from a cellar, in through a window above a porch. Run a generator outdoors only, and keep it at least 6 metres — 20 feet — from any door, window or vent, never in a garage, shed, porch or lean-to, even with the doors wide open [src: CDC — Carbon Monoxide Poisoning Prevention After an Emergency, 2021]. Never bring a barbecue or a charcoal tray inside, not into a house, a tent, a caravan or a garage [src: CDC — Carbon Monoxide Poisoning Prevention After an Emergency, 2021]. A camping stove used indoors needs real ventilation, a window genuinely open, and an adult standing over it.

Learn the tell. Carbon monoxide poisoning starts as a headache, dizziness, sickness, weakness and confusion, and it is easy to mistake for exhaustion or a bug [src: CDC — Carbon Monoxide Poisoning Prevention After an Emergency, 2021]. The give-away is that several people, and often the dog, feel ill at the same time and feel better outdoors. If that happens, everyone goes outside into fresh air immediately and nobody goes back in to fetch anything. Put a battery carbon monoxide alarm on every floor where people sleep, and test it today rather than adding it to a list.

Fire is the other half. A candle is a last resort and never a plan: a torch cannot fall over. If you do use a flame, keep it at least 30 cm from anything that can burn, never leave it alight in an empty room, and never carry one to bed [src: NFPA — Candle Safety, 2023]. Use a jar or a proper holder with a wide base, keep it away from curtains, paper and anything a cat could knock, and keep a bucket of water or sand nearby. Check the smoke alarms tonight, because a mains-powered alarm with a dead backup cell is now a plastic ornament.

### Light, and the discipline of the dark

Light is the first thing to run out and the thing people waste most. Ration it on the first evening, before scarcity teaches you to.

Give each person a head torch and make them responsible for it. Put one good lantern in the room the household is actually using, and leave the rest of the house dark — one lit room is warmer, more sociable and cheaper than four dim ones. Set the rule that the last person out of a room turns the light off, and enforce it on day zero while it is still a novelty.

Charge what you can, when you can, rather than when you need it. A small solar panel on a sunny sill will fill a set of rechargeable cells and a power bank in a day. A car will charge phones through its USB socket, but run the engine outdoors and only for as long as it takes, and never let the starting battery go flat, because a car that will not start is a car that is no longer a generator, a radio or a way to get to a hospital.

Then make the single biggest saving available to you: move onto daylight. Get up when it is light, do the heavy work in the morning, eat before dark, and go to bed early. A household that shifts its day by two hours burns half the light and sleeps better than one that fights the dark until midnight.

### People: who is missing, and who cannot manage

Count the household out loud. Who is in the house, who is out, where did they go, and when were they expected? Write it on the door sheet. Then agree the rule that saves the evening: if you are not back by dark, we assume you are sheltering somewhere safe and we do not come looking until morning. Nobody should be walking unlit lanes at ten at night looking for someone who is sitting comfortably in a friend's kitchen.

Agree a meeting point and a message drop — a neighbour's porch, a note under a specific stone, the village noticeboard — because phones are the first thing to fail and the last thing people stop trusting.

Then look outward, and do it today rather than when it gets difficult. The people who need a knock on day zero are the ones over seventy, anyone living alone, anyone with a new baby, and anyone running something on mains power that matters: an oxygen concentrator, a stairlift, a hoist, a nebuliser, a dialysis machine, or medicine that lives in the fridge. Insulin is the common one — keep it cool but never frozen, and a cool box with a wrapped bottle of frozen water in it will hold a sensible temperature far longer than a warm cupboard. Anyone dependent on mains-powered medical equipment needs a plan tonight, not on Wednesday, and that plan usually involves someone with a car.

Before dark, sit everyone down for ten minutes. Say what you know, say plainly what you do not know, and then set the house rules out loud: no flame left alone, drinking water is boiled or treated, doors shut, everything written on the sheet, and one job each tomorrow. A household meeting on day zero is worth more than any piece of equipment you own, because it converts a frightened group of people into a crew with jobs.

> **Day-X Challenge** — Day zero, tonight: from dusk until bedtime, switch the house off at the consumer unit and live the evening you have just planned. Cook, eat, wash up, put children to bed, and read by the light you actually own. You will find the missing batteries, the torch that does not work and the fact that nobody knows where the tin opener lives — and you will find them on an evening when you can still fix it.

### Information without the internet

The radio is the backbone of the next month. Get the national station's frequency, write it on the set with a strip of tape, and write it on the kitchen sheet as well, because the person who knows it will be asleep when it matters.

Listen at the top of the hour rather than continuously: bulletins repeat, batteries do not. Make one person the listener, and have them write what was said with the time beside it — actually write it, in a notebook kept in one place. Within three days you will have the only reliable record on the street, and you will use it to settle a dozen arguments.

Be ruthless with rumour. Write beside each item whether it was heard on the radio, seen with your own eyes, or told to you by somebody, and name them. Mark anything else as unconfirmed and treat it that way in conversation. In a week without the internet, the household that separates *seen* from *said* makes noticeably better decisions than the one that does not.

For phones: one handset stays on as the household phone and the rest go off, not on standby. Check it at agreed times — morning, midday and dusk is plenty — and send texts rather than making calls. A dead phone with nothing to charge it is a paperweight, and a single shared phone with a week of battery is a lifeline.

### The first night

The first night is about sleep, not vigilance. Tired people make the decisions that hurt them, so put the household to bed properly.

Bring everyone into one or two rooms and close the doors on the rest of the house. Draw the curtains at dusk to keep the day's warmth in. In cold weather, aim to keep the room people are in at or above 18 °C, and layer clothing and bedding rather than trying to heat the building [src: WHO — Housing and Health Guidelines, 2018]. Lock up as you normally would and no more: on day zero the risk is a fall in the dark, not a stranger.

Set out the night kit beside each bed — a torch, a bottle of drinking water and a pair of shoes — because the one thing you can guarantee is that somebody will get up in the dark. Leave the telltale lamp on. Put the day's paper log somewhere you will find it in the morning, and write tomorrow's first three jobs at the bottom of it before you go up.

> **Climate note (cold)** — In a hard frost the priority order changes: warmth moves ahead of everything but air. Shut off and drain what you can if the house will be unheated for days, keep taps dripping only where freezing pipes are a real risk, and never use an unflued fuel heater in a closed room. Put people in one room with a closed door, and use bodies, blankets and a hot water bottle before you use fuel.

### The morning of day one: the ledger and the rota

Day zero ends with three sheets of paper on the kitchen wall, and everything that follows hangs off them.

**The log.** Date, time, what happened, what was heard and who said it. One line each.

**The ledger.** What you actually have, counted rather than remembered: litres of water, days of food, batteries, fuel, candles, medicines. Count it once properly on day one and update it as things are used.

**The rota.** Names against jobs: water, fuel and fire, cooking, the information listener, the outdoor round, and the person who checks on neighbours. Give children real jobs with real names — Keeper of the Torches is a genuine post — and change the rota weekly so nobody carries the same chore for a month.

Then set the shape of the day: heavy work at first light, the main cooked meal in the afternoon while there is daylight to clean up by, and the evening for talk, cards and early bed. A household that writes things down and works to a rota beats a better-equipped household that relies on remembering, every single time. That is the whole of day zero: not a stockpile, a system.

## Thrive Hacks

**The jug lantern.** Stand a torch upright, beam pointing up, against a clear plastic bottle or a glass jug of water, and the whole thing turns into a soft lamp that lights a table instead of a spot on the ceiling. It is the difference between four people sitting in a gloom and four people playing cards. A white plastic bag over a lantern does the same job more roughly.

**Head torches on the door handle.** Hang one on the inside handle of every bedroom door on day zero. Nobody hunts for a light in the dark, ever again, and it costs nothing.

**The freezer becomes the cold box.** Once the freezer is losing its fight, move everything into one insulated box — a cool box, a cardboard box inside a duvet, a plastic crate wrapped in a sleeping bag — with your frozen water bottles packed around it. Concentrated cold lasts far longer than spread-out cold, and the bottles become drinking water as they melt.

**Cook once, eat warm three times.** Boil, then decant. A vacuum flask filled with boiling water in the morning is tea at eleven and a hot water bottle at four. A pot of stew wrapped in a towel inside a box of newspaper carries on cooking for hours with no fuel at all — the hay box is the oldest fuel-saving trick there is, and it works perfectly on day zero.

**Solar garden lights are indoor lights.** Every solar stake light in the garden is a free rechargeable lamp. Bring them in at dusk, stand them in jars on the table and down the hall, and put them back out at dawn. Four of them make a corridor safe all night for nothing.

**The doorstep noticeboard.** Pin a sheet of paper inside your front window facing out, and write the day's confirmed news, the time of the next radio bulletin and where you will be. Neighbours read it without knocking, you stop repeating yourself, and the person too proud to ask for help learns what is going on anyway.

**The daylight shift.** Move the whole household's clock to the sun on the very first evening rather than drifting into it over a fortnight. Early nights feel strange for two days and then feel normal, and you will save more light, fuel and temper than any gadget will give you.

**One comms window.** Agree that the household phone goes on for ten minutes at set times only. Everyone saves their messages for the window. Battery lasts days instead of hours, and nobody spends the evening staring at a dead signal bar.

**A pencil beats a printer.** Keep a soft pencil and a hand sharpener with every paper sheet in the house. Pens freeze, run out and fail on damp paper; a pencil writes upside down, in the rain, at minus five, and never needs a thing.
