# Chapter 6 · Health

> Almost everything that hurts a household in a dark month is preventable, and most of the rest is helped by clean hands, clean water and a calm plan.

[IMAGE: An ordinary kitchen table set up as a first-aid station — a lidded box of dressings, a covered jug of boiled water cooling, a bar of soap, a digital thermometer, a marked jug of rehydration drink, and a notebook open at a page of names]

## Do This Now

1. **Wash your hands, and build somewhere to keep doing it.** Soap and a jug by the door outperform every medicine in this chapter.
2. **Put all medicines in one lidded box**, somewhere cool, dark and out of reach of children, and write on the lid what is inside.
3. **Count the prescriptions.** For each person, how many days are left? Write the number down. It decides how early you start asking for help.
4. **Mix one litre of rehydration drink now** and taste it, so you can make it in the dark with a sick child on your hip. The recipe is in this chapter.
5. **Boil a jug of water and let it cool covered**, for washing wounds and mixing drinks. Then do it every morning.
6. **Write one health page per person**: age, conditions, medicines, allergies, and who to reach. Pin it inside a cupboard door.
7. **Name the sick room** before anyone is sick — the room you can spare, close and air out.
8. **Find out where the nearest nurse, midwife, paramedic, vet or trained first-aider lives.** Walk it in daylight, before you need it.

Then sit down and read the rest of this chapter once, tonight, while everybody is well.

## The Craft

This chapter is first aid and home care, and nothing more. It will not teach you to diagnose, prescribe or operate. What it will do is keep small things small, which is the whole game. In a grid-down month the illnesses that fill the beds are dull and preventable: diarrhoea, infected cuts, burns from unfamiliar cooking, heat and cold injuries, and the slow unravelling of people whose regular medicines have run out. Every one of those is a household job before it is a medical one.

Three questions carry you through any incident. Is this life-threatening right now? Can I make it better here, with what I have? Does it need someone with more training, and how do I reach them? Ask them in that order, out loud if it helps, and you will rarely do the wrong thing.

### The medicine that does most of the work

Soap. Not antiseptic, not tablets, not a clever kit. Hand-washing with plain soap, and about 20 seconds of rubbing before you rinse, is the highest-value medical act in the house [src: CDC — When and How to Wash Your Hands, 2022]. It happens before anyone eats or cooks, before touching a wound or a dressing, and after the latrine, the bins, the animals and the patient.

Four habits do the bulk of it:

| Habit | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Hands washed with soap, at the door and before food | Stops the illness that spreads fastest |
| Drinking water boiled or treated, always | See the water chapter; most household illness starts here |
| A latrine and a wash point kept clean and away from water | See the sanitation chapter |
| Wounds washed early and covered | Turns a scratch into a scratch, not an abscess |

None of it is interesting. All of it works, and it keeps working on days when nothing else is available.

### Wounds: wash early, watch closely

Most cuts in a dark month come from familiar jobs in unfamiliar conditions — a knife in bad light, a saw with tired hands, a nail in a scavenged board. Treat them properly the first time and they heal on their own.

1. **Stop the bleeding.** Firm, steady, direct pressure with the cleanest cloth you have, and keep it there. Time it by a clock rather than by feel, because everyone lifts the pad too early. Raise the limb if you can. Most bleeding stops.
2. **Clean your own hands** and put on gloves if you have them.
3. **Wash the wound out, generously.** Clean drinking-quality water in volume is the workhorse, and irrigating with plenty of clean water beats saving a small bottle of antiseptic for the moment [src: WHO — Prevention and Management of Wound Infection, 2016]. A squeeze bottle or a clean garden sprayer gives the gentle pressure that lifts grit out of a graze.
4. **Get the dirt out.** Grit, splinters, road grime and grass seeds are what cause infection. Tweezers, good light and patience. If something is deeply embedded, or you cannot see the bottom of the wound, stop and get help.
5. **Do not pour antiseptic into an open wound** or scrub it with a brush. Antiseptics belong on the intact skin around a wound; inside it they damage the tissue that has to heal.
6. **Cover it** with a dry, non-adherent dressing. Change it when it is wet, dirty or smelly, and otherwise leave it alone — a dressing peeled back hourly heals slowly.
7. **Watch it.** Check it once a day, in daylight, and write down what you see.

Small, clean, straight cuts can be pulled together with closure strips, leaving gaps so fluid escapes. Gaping wounds, wounds over a joint, deep punctures, animal and human bites and anything on the face want trained hands. Do not sew a wound shut at home: stitching in dirt builds an abscess with a lid on it. Punctures are washed out and left open, never sealed.

Learn the signs that a wound is going wrong, because you are the monitoring equipment: spreading redness, especially a red line tracking away from the wound; pain increasing after the second day instead of settling; heat, swelling and throbbing; pus or a bad smell; a fever appearing a day or two after the injury; tender lumps in the armpit or groin on that side. Any of those is the moment to spend a favour, a walk or a bicycle getting medical help.

Tetanus deserves its own line. It comes from dirty wounds, soil, manure and rust, it is preventable by vaccination, and it is very hard to treat. Know now whether every adult in the house is up to date, and treat a deep or soil-contaminated wound in an unvaccinated person as a reason to seek help rather than to shrug.

> **What I Wish I'd Stocked** — Not a pre-packed nylon pouch with a red cross on it. Instead: a shoebox of proper non-adherent dressings in three sizes, two crepe bandages, a triangular bandage, a roll of microporous tape, sterile gauze swabs, closure strips, blunt-nosed scissors, good tweezers, a box of nitrile gloves, a digital thermometer with spare batteries, two dozen oral rehydration sachets, and plain paracetamol and ibuprofen in their boxes with the leaflets still in. Bulk beats variety: you run out of gauze and tape long before you run out of anything clever.

### Burns: cool water, lots of it, straight away

Cooking moves to fires, camping stoves and candles in a blackout, and burns go up accordingly. The treatment is simple and people still get it wrong.

Cool the burn under cool or lukewarm running water for 20 minutes, and it is still worth doing up to three hours after the injury [src: NHS — Burns and scalds, 2023]. Twenty minutes is far longer than it feels, so use a clock. Where running water is scarce, a bowl refilled with cool water, or wet cloths swapped every minute or two, does the work.

While you cool it, take off rings, watches, bracelets and belts before the swelling starts, and remove clothing that is not stuck to the skin. Do not use ice, iced water, butter, oil, toothpaste, flour or egg white, and do not burst blisters. Once cooled, cover the burn with cling film laid along the limb in strips rather than wrapped tightly round it, or with a clean non-fluffy cloth, and keep the rest of the person warm, because a large burn cools the whole body. Give small, frequent drinks.

Get medical help for a burn larger than the casualty's own palm; any burn on the face, hands, feet, genitals or across a joint; any chemical or electrical burn; any burn that looks white, waxy, leathery or charred, or that is painless; and any burn in a baby, a young child or a frail older person [src: NHS — Burns and scalds, 2023]. For a chemical splash, on skin or in an eye, rinse with cool running water for at least 20 minutes, sweeping from the inner corner of the eye outwards, and keep going while somebody fetches help [src: NHS — Burns and scalds, 2023].

> **Climate note (hot)** — In heat, the health risks stack: people dehydrate while they work, food spoils faster, wounds go septic sooner and tempers shorten. Move heavy work to dawn and dusk, put a drinking station where the work is happening rather than in the kitchen, and check on the very old and the very young twice a day rather than waiting for them to complain. Thirst arrives late in both groups.

### Rehydration: the most useful thing in this book

If you learn one skill from this chapter, learn this one. For diarrhoea and vomiting, replacing fluid and salts by mouth matters more than any other treatment available in a home setting [src: WHO — The Treatment of Diarrhoea: A Manual for Physicians and Other Senior Health Workers, 2005]. It costs a jug of clean water and two kitchen ingredients, and it is what keeps a bout of illness from becoming a death.

Shop-bought oral rehydration salts are the best version. One sachet dissolves in one litre of clean water, and the modern reduced-osmolarity formula is the one to buy [src: WHO — Oral Rehydration Salts: Production of the New ORS, 2006]. Mix it fresh, keep it covered and cool, and throw away what is left after a day. Do not mix it into milk, fizzy drinks or juice, and do not make it stronger than the packet says.

When the sachets run out, the home solution is this:

| Ingredient | Amount in one litre of clean water | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Table salt | Half a level teaspoon, not a heaped one | [src: WHO — The Treatment of Diarrhoea: A Manual for Physicians and Other Senior Health Workers, 2005] |
| Sugar | Six level teaspoons | [src: WHO — The Treatment of Diarrhoea: A Manual for Physicians and Other Senior Health Workers, 2005] |
| Water | Boiled and cooled, or otherwise treated | See the water chapter |

Salt is the ingredient that hurts when you get it wrong, so measure it level and err weak rather than strong [src: WHO — The Treatment of Diarrhoea: A Manual for Physicians and Other Senior Health Workers, 2005]. The taste test is the old field check: it should be no saltier than tears. More sugar does not make it work better; it makes diarrhoea worse. With no sugar in the house, salted rice water or a thin salted cereal gruel does much the same job.

Giving it is a technique, not a gesture: small sips, or a spoonful at a time, every minute or two, patiently, for as long as it takes [src: WHO — The Treatment of Diarrhoea: A Manual for Physicians and Other Senior Health Workers, 2005]. A vomiting person can still absorb fluid between vomits, so wait ten minutes after a vomit and start again slower. Keep breastfeeding a baby, and keep offering food to anyone who will eat, because starving a gut does not rest it. The amount to aim for after each loose stool is worth knowing by heart:

| Age | Give after each loose stool | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Under two years | 50–100 ml | [src: WHO — The Treatment of Diarrhoea: A Manual for Physicians and Other Senior Health Workers, 2005] |
| Two to nine years | 100–200 ml | [src: WHO — The Treatment of Diarrhoea: A Manual for Physicians and Other Senior Health Workers, 2005] |
| Ten years and over | As much as they want | [src: WHO — The Treatment of Diarrhoea: A Manual for Physicians and Other Senior Health Workers, 2005] |

For children with diarrhoea, zinc shortens the illness and reduces the chance of it coming back: 20 mg a day for 10 to 14 days, and 10 mg a day for infants under six months [src: WHO — The Treatment of Diarrhoea: A Manual for Physicians and Other Senior Health Workers, 2005]. Zinc tablets are cheap, keep well and are worth having in the box.

Knowing when someone is drying out is the other half of the skill. In an adult, the early signs are thirst, a dry mouth and lips, dark strong-smelling urine, tiredness, dizziness on standing, and passing urine fewer than 4 times a day [src: NHS — Dehydration, 2023]. Urine the colour of pale straw means you are winning; the colour of tea means you are not.

In a baby or small child, look for sunken eyes, no tears when crying, a dry mouth, a sunken soft spot on the head, many fewer wet nappies than usual, and unusual sleepiness or floppiness [src: NHS — Dehydration, 2023]. The two field signs that matter most are the eyes and the skin pinch: pinch the skin of the abdomen and let go, and if it flattens slowly the child is dehydrated, and if it stays tented for seconds the child is severely dehydrated and needs urgent help [src: WHO — The Treatment of Diarrhoea: A Manual for Physicians and Other Senior Health Workers, 2005]. A drowsy, floppy child who will not drink, or anyone who has passed no urine at all for many hours, is an emergency, not a home-care case.

### Fever: treat the person, not the number

A fever is usually a temperature of 38 °C or above, and it is a sign the body is fighting something rather than a disease in itself [src: NHS — High temperature (fever) in adults, 2023]. A digital thermometer under the arm or in the mouth is worth its place in the box, and a written record of temperatures over a day tells you more than any single reading.

The home care is unglamorous: fluids offered constantly, rest, a light covering rather than a pile of blankets, a cool cloth if it comes as a comfort, and a room that is aired. Do not sponge a shivering person with cold water and do not bundle a hot child up.

For pain and discomfort in adults, paracetamol is the first choice: one or two 500 mg tablets, up to four times in 24 hours, with at least four hours between doses, and never more than eight 500 mg tablets in 24 hours [src: NHS — Paracetamol for adults, 2023]. Overdose is quietly dangerous and the damage is not obvious for days, so count what has been taken and write it on the patient's page — especially where cold and flu remedies, which often contain paracetamol as well, are in the house.

Ibuprofen is the alternative: one or two 200 mg tablets up to three times a day with or after food, and no more than 1,200 mg in 24 hours without medical advice [src: NHS — Ibuprofen for adults, 2023]. Skip it, and use paracetamol instead, if the person is dehydrated or vomiting, has a history of stomach ulcers or bleeding, has asthma made worse by it, has kidney trouble, or is pregnant. Never give aspirin to a child under 16 [src: NHS — Aspirin for pain relief, 2023]. For children, doses go strictly by age and weight, so use the bottle's own scale and the packet's age bands and never scale an adult tablet by eye.

Some fevers are not home-care fevers. Get help, and keep trying, for a baby under three months with a temperature of 38 °C or above, or a baby three to six months at 39 °C or above [src: NHS — High temperature (fever) in children, 2023]; a stiff neck, severe headache and dislike of light; a rash that does not fade when you press a glass against it; a seizure; laboured or very fast breathing; a fever with confusion or unusual drowsiness; a fever that keeps climbing past a few days; or a fever in someone who is pregnant, on chemotherapy, or otherwise immune-suppressed. In malaria or dengue country, a fever is a walk to a clinic and a test, not a night of paracetamol.

> **Day-X Challenge** — Day nine, after dark. Someone calls out that a child has been vomiting for two hours. Without switching on a single mains light, find your medical box, mix a litre of rehydration drink correctly, take and write down a temperature, and lay out what you would need for the next six hours of nursing. Time yourself. Most households discover the thermometer has no battery, the sachets are in a different bag from the jug, and nobody can find a pen.

### Diarrhoea and vomiting

Assume it will happen. In most cases the treatment is the one above — fluid, salts, patience, and ruthless hand-washing so it stops with one person instead of touring the house. Keep the patient's cup, plate and towel separate, and wash them yourself.

Anti-diarrhoeal tablets such as loperamide have a narrow place: adults with ordinary travellers' diarrhoea who need to function. They are not for children, and not for anyone with blood in the stool or a high fever, where slowing the gut down makes matters worse [src: NHS — Loperamide, 2023]. They treat the symptom and do nothing about the fluid you have lost, so they never replace the drink.

Resist the urge to take antibiotics for a stomach upset. Most cases are viral, antibiotics will not touch them, a half-finished course breeds resistant bacteria in your own household, and you will want those tablets to exist and work later. Antibiotics are a clinician's decision, and the fact that a clinician is two days' walk away does not change the pharmacology.

Get help, or get moving towards it, for blood in the stool or vomit, vomiting that will not stop and prevents drinking at all, severe dehydration, no urine for many hours, a swollen and rigid abdomen, or profuse watery diarrhoea that comes by the litre rather than the cupful — that last one empties an adult frighteningly fast and is beyond home care [src: WHO — The Treatment of Diarrhoea: A Manual for Physicians and Other Senior Health Workers, 2005].

### Heat, cold and the weather inside the house

Heat exhaustion looks like heavy sweating, pale clammy skin, headache, cramps, dizziness and feeling sick. Move the person somewhere cool, lie them down, raise their feet a little, loosen clothing, cool the skin with water and fanning, and give them a rehydration drink or plain water in sips. They should start to improve within 30 minutes [src: NHS — Heat exhaustion and heatstroke, 2023].

If they do not improve, or they are confused, unsteady, have stopped sweating, have very hot dry skin, are breathing fast, or reach a temperature of 40 °C, treat it as heatstroke and an emergency: keep cooling them aggressively with water and air while you send for help, and do not try to make an unconscious person drink [src: NHS — Heat exhaustion and heatstroke, 2023].

Cold works the other way, and in an unheated house it works slowly enough that nobody notices. Hypothermia begins with shivering, cold pale skin, tiredness and clumsiness, and gets dangerous when the shivering stops and confusion, slurred speech and drowsiness set in; it is a body temperature below 35 °C [src: NHS — Hypothermia, 2023]. Handle the person gently, get them somewhere sheltered, replace wet clothing with dry, insulate them from the ground, cover the head, and add warmth slowly with blankets, another body's heat and warm sweet drinks if they are fully awake. Do not rub their limbs, do not put them in a hot bath, and do not give alcohol. Babies, thin people and the very old lose heat fastest and complain least.

> **Climate note (wet)** — Damp is a health problem, not a comfort problem. Constantly wet feet crack, macerate and go septic, and fungal rashes spread through a household on shared towels. Enforce one dry pair of socks per person kept out of general circulation, dry between the toes properly, air boots beside the stove every night, and treat a hot spot on a heel as an injury before it becomes a hole. Damp bedding steals more warmth than an open window.

### When someone collapses

This is the part to get trained in properly, before anything happens, by a real course with a plastic torso. What follows is a reminder, not a substitute.

Check for danger to you first, then shout their name and shake their shoulders, then shout for help. Open the airway by tilting the head back and lifting the chin, and look, listen and feel for normal breathing for no more than ten seconds. Occasional gasping is not normal breathing.

If they are breathing, put them in the recovery position on their side, keep them warm, and keep checking. If they are not breathing normally, start chest compressions immediately: heel of the hand in the centre of the chest, hard and fast, at a rate of 100 to 120 a minute and a depth of about 5 to 6 cm in an adult, giving 2 rescue breaths after every 30 compressions if you are trained and willing to [src: NHS — First aid: CPR, 2023]. Compressions alone are far better than nothing. It is exhausting work, so put a second person beside you and swap every couple of minutes.

For choking, if the person cannot cough, speak or breathe, give up to 5 sharp blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand, then up to 5 abdominal thrusts, and keep alternating while somebody fetches help [src: NHS — Choking, 2023]. For a baby, back blows and chest thrusts, never abdominal thrusts.

For a seizure, do not restrain the person and do not put anything in their mouth. Move furniture away, cushion the head, note the time it starts, and put them on their side once it stops. A seizure lasting more than five minutes, a second one straight after, a first-ever seizure, or a seizure in pregnancy needs urgent medical help.

### Sprains, strains and suspected breaks

Work injuries rise when the power goes off, and most of them are joints rather than bones. Rest it, cool it, support it with a firm but not tight bandage, and raise it. A cool pack held on for up to 20 minutes every 2 to 3 hours helps in the first days, and a cloth wrung out in cold stream water and swapped often does the job when there is no ice [src: NHS — Sprains and strains, 2023]. Never put ice straight onto skin.

If you suspect a break, the aim is to stop it moving and get the person to help, not to fix it. Pad generously, splint across the joints above and below the injury with anything rigid — kindling, a rolled magazine, cardboard, a walking stick — and tie it off away from the injury itself. Check the fingers or toes beyond the splint every so often: they should stay warm, pink and able to feel a touch, and if they do not, loosen everything. A sling from a triangular bandage or the tail of a shirt takes the weight off an arm and takes most of the pain with it. Do not try to straighten a deformed limb. An open break, a limb that looks wrong, numbness, a cold pale foot or hand, a suspected hip, spine or neck injury, or any head injury with drowsiness, repeated vomiting or a change in behaviour is a get-help-now problem.

### The medicine chest, and the prescriptions that matter more

Stock boring things in bulk rather than exotic things in ones.

| Group | What to keep | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Rehydration | Oral rehydration sachets, zinc tablets | The highest-value item in the box |
| Pain and fever | Paracetamol, ibuprofen, a child's liquid paracetamol | Keep the boxes and the leaflets |
| Skin and wounds | Non-adherent dressings, gauze, crepe and triangular bandages, tape, plasters, closure strips, antiseptic for intact skin, cling film | Bulk, in three sizes |
| Tools | Digital thermometer and batteries, blunt scissors, tweezers, nitrile gloves, torch, safety pins | A thermometer with a dead battery is a paperweight |
| Comfort | Antihistamine tablets and cream, hydrocortisone cream, antifungal cream, rehydration for after-effects, antacid, throat lozenges, eye wash | Small illnesses, big morale effect |
| Reference | This chapter, printed, plus a proper first-aid manual | Paper does not need charging |

The prescriptions are the real problem, and they are the part people forget while buying plasters. Insulin, inhalers, blood thinners, heart and blood-pressure medicines, epilepsy medicines, thyroid replacement and psychiatric medicines are not optional, and several of them are dangerous to stop suddenly. Ask your pharmacy now, in ordinary times, how far ahead you are allowed to order, keep the written list of names and doses on the person's health page, and count the days left on day one of any outage. Never share prescription medicines between people, however similar the complaint.

Store everything cool, dark and dry, in original boxes with the leaflets, because the leaflet is the source of truth for a dose you are unsure about. Heat is what degrades medicines fastest, so the medicine box does not live in a loft or beside the stove. Tablets kept cool and dry lose their strength slowly; liquids, insulin, and adrenaline auto-injectors are the ones genuinely worth keeping in date. Anything that has changed colour, smell or texture goes out. And resist stockpiling antibiotics for a rainy day: the wrong drug at the wrong dose for the wrong illness does harm, and the resistance it breeds is yours to keep.

> **Village Hack** — Do a street skills-and-stocks round on the first week. You are looking for two things: trained hands and spare supplies. In almost any lane there is a retired nurse, a veterinary nurse, a lifeguard, an ex-army medic, a dentist, a midwife or somebody who does first aid at work — and they usually do not volunteer it. Write the names and doors on one card, kept by two households. Then pool the boring stock, keep it in one dry box in an agreed place, and let one person own it. A village with a list of who knows what, and one shared box of dressings, is transformed for the price of an afternoon's knocking.

### The sick room, and not passing it on

The moment someone in the house has a fever or the runs, decide where they will be and who will look after them. One carer if you can manage it, and ideally not the person cooking for everybody.

Give the patient their own cup, plate, spoon and towel, and keep them apart from the household wash-up. Air the room properly, in every weather, by opening a window wide for a short spell and then closing it, which changes the air without chilling the patient. Wash hands on the way into the room and on the way out, and put the soap and water where you will actually use it. Handle soiled bedding as little as possible, wash it hot or boil it if you can, and dry it in sun where there is sun. Deal with waste and washing water as Chapter 7 describes, well away from any drinking source.

Keep a simple chart on the door: times, temperatures, drinks given, whether they have passed urine, and what medicine was taken when. It turns your nursing from guesswork into information, it prevents the double-dose that happens when two people are caring, and if you do reach a clinician it makes the handover in thirty seconds.

### The people to watch

Babies and small children dehydrate fast and go downhill faster than an adult, and then recover remarkably quickly once fluid is going in. Older people get cold, get dizzy, fall, and get confused when they are dehydrated or their medicines are disrupted, and they will tell you they are fine. Pregnant women need the midwife's name and door written down in week one, plus a clean plan for a birth that may happen without transport. Anyone with a long-term condition needs their days-of-medicine number checked weekly, out loud, by somebody other than themselves.

### When you cannot get help at all

Sometimes there will be nobody. What you can still do matters: rehydrate relentlessly, keep a fever comfortable, wash and dress wounds, keep the person warm, dry, upright or lying as suits them, keep the room aired, keep offering food and drink, and write everything down. Nursing well is not a small contribution — it is most of what recovery needs.

What you should not do is improvise medicine you do not understand: stitching wounds closed, cutting into infections, giving somebody else's prescription, guessing at antibiotics, or pouring a remedy from the internet into an open wound. And carry the honest line without pretending otherwise: some things cannot be fixed at home, being prepared is not the same as being able, and doing the simple things calmly and well is not a lesser kind of care.

## Thrive Hacks

**The nursing tray.** One tray, permanently assembled: a covered jug, two cups, a teaspoon, a cloth, a thermometer, the chart, a pencil and a small torch. When someone gets ill at two in the morning you pick up the tray instead of assembling a hospital in the dark.

**Mark the litre line on a jug.** Once, in daylight, with nail varnish or a file scratch, so the rehydration recipe works by feel with no measuring jug and no light. Scratch the half line too, and write the recipe on the jug itself in permanent marker. It is the single best five minutes you will spend on health preparation.

**A garden sprayer is a wound-irrigation kit.** A new pump-up sprayer, never used for chemicals, filled with boiled cooled water, gives you a controlled jet that lifts grit out of a graze with far less water than a running tap and far less pain than a scrubbing brush.

**Boiled cotton dressings.** When manufactured gauze runs out, clean cotton sheeting cut into squares, boiled, dried in sun and pressed with a hot iron, then kept in a sealed bag, is a workable dressing. Non-fluffy, plain-weave, and never anything with a nap that sheds fibres into a wound.

**Honey on a shallow, clean wound.** Honey has a long record as a wound dressing and hospitals still use a medical-grade version of it. As a field fallback on a small, clean, already-washed graze or minor burn, under a clean cover, it is better than nothing and better than a dirty dressing. It is not a substitute for cleaning, and not for deep or dirty wounds.

**Steam and salt water.** A bowl of hot water on the floor, a towel over the head, and ten minutes of breathing does real good for a blocked nose and a cough — on the floor, never on a lap, and never with a child unsupervised. Warm salty water gargled is the same kind of cheap relief for a sore throat.

**Rehydration lollies.** If anything in the house still freezes, rehydration drink frozen in a small mould or a bottle cap goes into a reluctant child when a cup will not. A sick child who will lick will usually drink afterwards.

**The notebook is an instrument.** Temperatures, drinks and urine written down over a day show you a direction, which is the thing you actually need. Trends beat snapshots, and a written trend is the only way to spot slow improvement and stop panicking at four in the morning.

**Teach a child first aid.** Give the nine-year-old the hand-washing rota, the thermometer and the job of teaching the little ones. They take it more seriously than adults, they enforce it mercilessly, and you have just doubled your trained hands.

**A way to move a person.** Two poles and a buttoned coat make a stretcher; a wheelbarrow with a folded duvet in it moves someone who cannot walk, badly but effectively. Work it out on a well day, with a willing volunteer, rather than on the night.
